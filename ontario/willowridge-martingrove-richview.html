Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, certain basement waterproofing-related tasks can trigger permit requirements, especially when you’re making changes that affect structural elements or lot drainage. In general, foundation excavation, structural crack repair where the integrity of a foundation wall may be impacted, and changes to how the lot drains (for example, re-routing discharge, connecting new drainage components, or significant grading changes) typically require a building permit. If your sump pump discharge connects to a storm or sanitary service, municipal approval is commonly required.

For structural crack repair—particularly horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracks, or anything suggesting movement—an assessment by a structural engineer is often needed to confirm whether underpinning or other structural work is required. In those cases, your waterproofing contractor should tell you whether engineering support is included or subcontracted.

How to verify contractor readiness in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview (step-by-step):

  • Confirm the contractor is properly licensed for the work they’re marketing and pulling required permits where applicable (ask who pulls the permit and provide the permit number once issued).
  • Request a current certificate of liability insurance and ensure it’s active for the project dates.
  • Ask for WSIB clearance/coverage details (or WCB documentation, depending on the contractor’s setup) and verify it’s current.
  • If structural repair is in scope, ask who provides the engineering assessment and whether they carry professional liability or coordinate it through a stamped report.
  • For discharge changes, request documentation of municipal approvals/requirements before installation.

Always verify these items in writing before work begins—right when a contractor can still clarify scope exclusions and permit responsibilities.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview need?

Interior and exterior waterproofing differ most in whether they stop water at the source or manage it after it gets through. Exterior waterproofing usually means full excavation, new membrane, replacement drainage tile, and then backfilling and reinstating landscaping. This approach permanently addresses hydrostatic pressure and bulk water entry—so it’s the best match when clay-heavy soils and higher local water load are consistently pushing against the foundation. The trade-off is disruption: excavation around mature landscaping and established driveways is time-consuming and can raise labour and disposal costs.

Interior waterproofing manages water after it enters the basement. Common systems include a perimeter drain channel, a sump pit, and a sump pump (often with a backup system). This is less invasive, frequently a practical solution when exterior access is constrained or when the visible leak pathways are isolated cracks and weeper failures. That said, interior work does not relieve hydrostatic pressure against the wall itself, so it must be sized correctly for peak seepage—especially during spring when freeze–thaw drives water movement.

In Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview, poured concrete walls often respond well to crack injection when the crack pattern is stable, because concrete generally seals more predictably when properly cleaned and sealed. Block foundations, meanwhile, can require interior drainage as a practical complement, because water can travel through block voids and mortar joints even when some cracks look minor. If you’re seeing dampness plus efflorescence, interior drainage plus a sump is often the more reliable “get control now” solution, while exterior work is considered when source pressure is clearly persistent.

Dollar reality: a full exterior excavation can sit around $15,000–$25,000. If your issue is confined to one or two active cracks, crack injection alone may fit in the $600–$3,000 range, and you avoid major landscape disruption. But if the weeping tile is widely failed and water keeps migrating along multiple wall segments, the interior-only plan can get expensive in repeat service calls—making exterior source control more justified.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview

Choosing the right contractor in Willowridge-Martingrove-Richview starts with proof: licensing (where applicable for the type of work), current liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB coverage for employees. To check each item, ask for documents before signing. For insurance, request a certificate of insurance showing liability coverage limits that match basement waterproofing risks (work around excavation and electrical connections). For WSIB/WCB, ask for a clearance letter or current status confirmation from the provider so you can see it’s active on the job dates. Don’t accept “we’re covered” without paperwork—especially when excavation is involved.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes. You want a breakdown that separates labour, materials (membrane, drainage tile, pipe, pump), electrical components, disposal, and any engineering or discharge testing. Avoid lump-sum estimates that don’t show what’s included. Confirm whether a permit is required and whether the contractor pulls it, and whether disposal/reinstatement (topsoil, sod, deck base repairs) is included or billed separately.

Warranty matters in Ontario’s freeze–thaw climate. Ask for two layers: a workmanship warranty (how many years on installation) and product/manufacturer warranty on membranes and pumps. Also ask whether warranties are transferable to future owners—helpful if you sell. Payment should be staged: never pay more than about 10–15% upfront. Hold back part of the contract until completion, final walkthrough, and pump testing (including backup system testing if included).

Finally, insist on a written start date and completion estimate. A detailed scope reduces change orders and ensures the solution matches your soil and foundation type.

  • Request pump specs: discharge line size, check valve type, and high-water alarm options.

  • Ensure the quote notes exclusions (existing mould remediation, structural engineer fees, underpinning).
  • Keep payments aligned with milestones; holdback until final performance check.
