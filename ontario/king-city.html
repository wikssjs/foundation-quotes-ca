Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, certain foundation excavation and drainage changes generally require a building permit, especially when work touches structural elements, alters foundation openings, or changes how the lot directs water. As a rule of thumb for King City homeowners: foundation excavation and new drainage configurations near the foundation typically fall under permit requirements, and structural crack repair can trigger permit needs when the crack suggests movement or when repairs go beyond standard patching.

Sump pump installations that connect to the storm or sanitary sewer usually need municipal approval before discharge. If your existing drainage tie-ins are being altered, ask the contractor to confirm whether approvals are required for your specific discharge method and whether they handle the paperwork or coordinate it.

For structural crack repairs—commonly horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracks, or any crack pattern that suggests movement—it’s common to involve a structural engineer’s assessment to determine whether underpinning or other structural measures are needed. In those cases, you should verify the contractor has engineering support and liability coverage appropriate for the scope.

To verify a contractor in King City, start with licensing/registry checks using Ontario’s online resources for the contractor category they perform (and any required qualifications). Then request a certificate of insurance (liability) and confirm WSIB/WCB clearance or coverage for workers. Finally, if the contractor proposes structural repairs, ask for documentation of engineer involvement and ensure it aligns with the permit/approval path before any demolition begins.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does King City need?

The fundamental difference is that exterior waterproofing addresses the source of water entry by excavating to the foundation, installing a new membrane, and rebuilding drainage tile/backfill. Interior waterproofing manages water after it enters by collecting seepage with a perimeter drain channel and directing it to a sump pit and sump pump. Both approaches can stop a wet basement, but only exterior work can reliably reduce hydrostatic pressure at the wall—particularly in clay-heavy pockets where saturated soils exert lateral forces and where freeze–thaw keeps opening defects.

In King City’s Toronto-area climate, freeze–thaw cycles are a big reason we see “slow leaks” escalate. For poured concrete walls, crack injection (when cracks are non-moving and correctly identified) can be a high-value complement because concrete often responds well to properly prepped sealants. For block foundations, I often recommend interior drainage as a practical complement because water can travel through mortar joints and micro-pathways even when surface sealing looks good. If your seepage is chronic during spring recharge, a sump pump system becomes the most direct control point.

Ontario power reliability also matters. A battery backup sump pump is especially valuable when spring storms combine with outages—because even a short interruption can let water rise quickly in the perimeter. A well-sized interior system with backup can be the most cost-effective way to protect valuables and finish levels without committing to full excavation.

Where the price difference is justified: for example, if you’re dealing with failed original drainage and active wall seepage around multiple sides, exterior waterproofing can land in the $7,000–$25,000 range. In contrast, a targeted interior perimeter drain plus sump strategy commonly falls in the $3,500–$15,000 band—often enough if your goal is to stop seepage while you plan for long-term source control.

Ask for documents before signing anything, and verify the coverage dates align with the job schedule.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes. “Itemised” should mean separate lines for labour, materials (membranes, drain tile, pumps, sealants), disposal/backfill, and any concrete demo/repairs. Ask whether the quote includes permits (if required), protected work areas, and disposal of excavated material. Clarify what’s excluded: patching only, finishing (paint/drywall), restoring landscaping, electrical work for pumps/backup, and any engineer reviews.

Warranty matters in waterproofing because failures often appear after a winter cycle. Ask for the workmanship warranty length, the product/manufacturer warranty details (and what conditions void it), and whether the warranty is transferable to future owners. Payment structure should protect you: never pay more than 10–15% upfront; request a holdback until completion and commissioning/testing (for example, pump tests and discharge confirmation). Finally, require a written start date and a realistic completion estimate; in King City, exterior timelines depend heavily on excavation access, weather windows, and backfill conditions.

A battery backup sump pump is a secondary pump system designed to run during power outages when the primary pump loses electricity. In King City, spring high-water and storm events can coincide with outages, and interior systems rely on uninterrupted pumping to prevent water accumulation. Neither is universally “better”—the right choice depends on how water is entering. Why is my basement leaking in King City?

Basement leaks in King City usually come from water pressure and drainage issues, not one single cause. Common Toronto-region culprits include dense clay soils that hold water and expand during freeze–thaw, increasing pressure against foundation walls and worsening cracks over time. Older drainage systems—such as early weeping tile—may also be partially collapsed or clogged, leading to dampness, efflorescence, and eventually active seepage. Another frequent cause is surface water: heavy rain runoff that isn’t properly directed away from the foundation. If you only have dampness near a crack or corner, targeted crack injection may help; if you have widespread water, interior drainage and sump pumping are often needed. A proper assessment should identify whether it’s crack-driven, joint-driven, or water-table related.

How do I know if a foundation crack is serious?

Not all foundation cracks are the same, and in King City the difference often shows up in crack pattern and whether the crack is active. Hairline cracks that are stable over time may be suitable for epoxy sealing, while active leaks through cracks typically call for polyurethane injection. Cracks that are wider, show horizontal movement, or form step patterns in block walls often signal structural concern and should be evaluated—sometimes by a structural engineer—to decide if underpinning or additional work is needed. Also watch for signs like wetness during storms, recurring water at the same spot, efflorescence, or dampness that returns after freeze–thaw periods. If you’re planning repairs, request a scope that includes proper crack evaluation before selecting the sealing system.
