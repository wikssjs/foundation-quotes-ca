Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Amherstburg need?

The fundamental difference is simple: exterior waterproofing treats the water at its source by excavating to the foundation, applying a continuous membrane, installing/renewing drainage tile, and backfilling with proper drainage material. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—manages water after it enters the basement. In the Amherstburg context, exterior solutions are often the most permanent because Windsor–Sarnia soils can stay wet near the foundation and seasonal groundwater can build hydrostatic pressure against walls.

That said, exterior isn’t always justified. In older Amherstburg neighbourhood pockets where landscaping, driveways, or additions limit excavation, interior drainage is frequently the practical best value. For poured concrete walls, we more often see crack injection or interior sealing paired with proper interior drainage because the wall tends to respond well to sealing non-moving defects; for block foundations, interior drainage is commonly necessary because seepage paths through joints and capillaries can keep reappearing unless the perimeter is managed. Freeze–thaw also matters: foundation movement can make “seal-only” solutions fail, so crack type and history decide whether exterior membrane or interior drainage is the stronger play.

Power reliability is another reason to consider backup. During spring wet periods, if the sump pump is your active line of defence, a battery backup or equivalent can prevent a basement flood when electricity is lost.

For a dollar example: if your contractor offers an exterior plan in the $12,000–$25,000 band but your site access is tight and the water entry is mostly through a known interior seep point, you might see better value in a combined interior perimeter drain and sump approach in the $8,000–$18,000 band—provided the system is designed around the water table behaviour and discharge pathway.

Frequently asked questions — waterproofing in Amherstburg

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — which is better?

For Amherstburg homes, exterior waterproofing is usually the “best” long-term solution because it addresses water entry: excavation, membrane coverage, proper drainage tile, and drainage backfill. Interior waterproofing is typically the best choice when you can’t excavate exterior walls due to driveways, decks, tight lot lines, or occupied homes where disruption must be limited. The Windsor–Sarnia soil and seasonal groundwater behaviour often means hydrostatic pressure keeps finding paths at the foundation face, which is why exterior can outperform when access allows. In costs, you’ll often see exterior ranges around $12,000–$25,000, while interior drainage and sump projects often fall closer to $8,000–$18,000. The better answer depends on your crack type, where water appears first (walls vs floor edges), and whether regrading alone would actually solve the problem.

Why is my basement leaking in Amherstburg?

In Amherstburg, basement leaks commonly happen because groundwater and surface water combine to create persistent moisture against the foundation. When fine-textured soils hold water and the water table rises seasonally, seepage can push through cracks, joints, and mortar areas—especially in housing built before 1981 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census). Freeze–thaw can also widen existing pathways: small cracks and seal failures grow over multiple winter cycles. Often, older homes have aging or clogged weeping tile, so interior symptoms show up even when exterior damp-proofing looks “fine.” Another frequent cause is surface runoff: if downspouts discharge close to the foundation or grading slopes toward the house, water saturates the backfill and reaches the wall faster. The most accurate way to confirm the cause is a site inspection with crack mapping and review of drainage/discharge conditions.

How do I know if a foundation crack is serious?

A crack is more concerning when it suggests movement or structural behaviour, rather than simple shrinkage. In Amherstburg, pay attention to horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracks that climb and shift between courses, cracks that widen seasonally, or evidence of bowing at wall corners. Also take note of “active leak” signs: dampness around the crack, water seepage during spring thaws, and mineral staining or efflorescence that keeps returning. Hairline cracks that remain dry and stable for years can sometimes be sealed after proper assessment. But the safest approach is to document the crack (measure width, take photos, note timing with rain/snow melt) and have a contractor classify it. If it appears structural, an engineer assessment may be required before any waterproofing strategy—because stopping water without addressing movement can lead to repeat failures.

How much does foundation crack repair cost in Amherstburg?

For Amherstburg, foundation crack repair pricing usually depends on crack type, length, and whether there’s active leakage. Typical local ranges for crack injection are about $600–$2,500. Epoxy injection is commonly used for non-moving cracks (often cheaper because it’s straightforward), while polyurethane injection is used when there’s active leakage or flexible movement and can run toward the upper end due to prep and material handling. Costs also rise if we must address more than one crack line, drill additional ports, or prepare the surface with cleaning/etching so the product bonds properly. If the crack indicates structural movement or there are multiple failure modes (for example, water pressure plus joint failure), you should expect that waterproofing may involve drainage upgrades as well, not just injection.

Do I need a sump pump in Amherstburg?

You may need a sump pump in Amherstburg when seepage consistently reaches a level where interior drainage alone can’t manage it, or when the foundation experiences seasonal groundwater pressure. Interior perimeter drains often work with a sump pit to collect seepage and keep the basement drier. If your basement is affected during spring flooding periods or you’ve seen water accumulate near the wall line or floor perimeter, a sump system becomes a practical control. Because power outages can occur, adding a backup (battery or equivalent) is often worth considering when a sump is the main line of defence. Sump installation commonly falls around $1,800–$4,500, depending on pump selection and whether backup is included. That said, if your leak is primarily from surface water, re-grading/downspouts may be the better starting point—so it’s important to match the system to the root cause.

How does Amherstburg's soil affect my foundation?

In Amherstburg and the Windsor–Sarnia corridor, soil type is a major determinant of waterproofing performance. Many areas feature fine-textured, poorly drained soils that can retain moisture and keep the backfill saturated near the foundation. That means hydrostatic pressure can build against basement walls, pushing water through cracks and joints. Clay-heavy soils also expand during freeze–thaw cycles, which can widen gaps over time and turn previously minor seepage pathways into active leaks. Even in sandier pockets, seasonal groundwater movement can still transmit moisture toward basements when the water table rises. Homes built earlier (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census) often rely on older drainage systems that are prone to failure, such as clogged weeping tile or inadequate exterior drainage. The practical takeaway: your soil conditions influence whether exterior drainage/membrane is the primary solution or whether interior drainage and sump controls are the right fit.
