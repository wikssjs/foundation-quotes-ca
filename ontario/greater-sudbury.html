Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, waterproofing projects can range from minor sealing to structural-related repairs, and the permit requirement depends on what you’re changing. In general, foundation excavation near a structure, structural crack repair that addresses movement, and changes to lot drainage often require a building permit. If the work modifies foundation elements or involves structural assessments (for example, major step cracks in block walls or horizontal cracks suggesting movement), you should expect additional oversight.

Sump pump installations that discharge to municipal services (storm or sanitary) typically require municipal approval. If discharge is rerouted to daylight or to a new approved discharge point, the permitting pathway can still apply depending on where the discharge goes.

For structural crack repair—particularly horizontal cracks in block walls or major step cracks—an engineer’s assessment is often needed to determine whether underpinning or other structural measures are required. When you request a quote, ask whether the contractor provides engineering support for structural scenarios, and confirm they carry liability insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage.

How to verify from your Greater Sudbury home: first, ask for the contractor’s business registration details and the Ontario licence information where applicable; second, request a certificate of insurance (liability) and confirm the policy is active and that you’re not being asked to assume risk; third, ask for WSIB/WCB clearance documentation. If a contractor can’t provide these items promptly, treat it as a red flag and request written proof before signing.

Tip: Ask the contractor to clearly state which permits they will pull (and who is responsible for what) in the written scope.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Greater Sudbury need?

The fundamental difference is source control versus symptom management. Exterior waterproofing involves excavating around the foundation, installing a new waterproofing membrane, adding or replacing perimeter drainage tile, and then carefully backfilling. Done correctly, it permanently reduces water pressure at the wall. It’s also more disruptive because it requires exterior access and later landscaping restoration.

Interior waterproofing—such as a perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—captures water after it enters the basement and conveys it to the sump for removal. It’s typically less invasive and can be staged to limit disruption to finished basements, but it does not stop hydrostatic pressure acting on the wall. In Greater Sudbury, where freeze–thaw can widen cracks over time, interior solutions work best when paired with crack injection for the right crack types and when drainage capacity is properly sized.

Foundation type helps decide the practical approach. Poured concrete walls often respond well to targeted crack injection (especially when leakage is through defined cracks), while block foundations frequently need a more comprehensive interior drainage system because water can travel through mortar joints and multiple pathways. If weeping tile is already failed, relying on interior only can still keep the basement dry, but you must plan for long-term pumping and, in many cases, battery backup.

Here’s a concrete pricing example: homeowners sometimes compare a crack injection-only approach (often roughly $400 – $1,200) against full exterior work (about $9,000 – $20,000). Injection can be the right call for a localized seep, but if you’re seeing recurring wetness after spring thaw along multiple walls, exterior waterproofing is more justified because it removes the cause rather than chasing leaks season after season.

What drainage issues are most common in Greater Sudbury homes?

The most common problems we see are water pooling near foundation corners, clogged or failed weeping tile, and cracks/joints opened by freeze–thaw. Many homes built in earlier decades relied on drainage methods that can age out over time—so even if the basement was “dry” for years, saturated soil around the foundation can eventually overwhelm older drainage pathways. Once water finds a route, it can show up as intermittent wet drywall seams, tide-like seepage, or mineral deposits along the base.

From a homeowner’s perspective, look for patterns: wetness that peaks after heavy rain or rapid snowmelt often points to surface water or perimeter drainage failure. If you’re seeing water around window wells or downspouts discharge near the footing, that’s another frequent cause. Interior solutions (perimeter drains and sump systems) can help, but without fixing surface grading, you may see recurring loads during spring flooding seasons.

What is a battery backup sump pump and do I need one in Greater Sudbury?

A battery backup sump pump is an additional pumping system that can run during a power outage, so the basement doesn’t flood if municipal power drops during heavy spring rain or wind events. In Greater Sudbury, power reliability can be tested during storms and rapid melt periods, when groundwater inflow to the sump can rise quickly. A backup is most valuable when you already have signs of active seepage, a functioning sump that runs frequently, or a history of basement water during outages.

Do you “need” it? If your plan is interior drainage with a sump, it’s a strong risk-management upgrade—particularly for finished basements, families with vulnerable areas (storage, laundry, utilities), or homes with known high meltwater response. Many homeowners pair it with the sump installation budget, which is commonly around $900 – $3,000 depending on system components and discharge routing.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — which is better?

Neither is automatically “better”—they’re different solutions to different realities. Exterior waterproofing is best when the problem is consistent hydrostatic pressure against the wall or when older exterior drainage (like original weeping tile) has failed. It addresses the source by installing a membrane and proper drainage around the foundation, but it costs more and disrupts landscaping/driveways. Interior waterproofing is typically better when you need a less invasive retrofit, can’t access the exterior, or when the foundation wall is mainly letting water in through known crack pathways and you’ll manage the water with drains and a sump.

In Greater Sudbury’s freeze–thaw climate, interior-only approaches still work well when properly designed, but they must match inflow conditions. A common example: crack injection alone (often $400 – $1,200) may not be sufficient if the basement stays wet after spring melt—where a perimeter drain and sump solution in the $4,000 – $12,000 band may be the more complete fix.
