What affects the price of waterproofing in Duncan

In Duncan, it’s common to see the “same” basement waterproofing problem quoted 30–50% apart across Vancouver Island and Coast and other parts of British Columbia. On Vancouver Island and the Coast, exterior systems often cost more because excavation, membrane labour, and perimeter drainage are more intensive when drainage fails and the surrounding soil stays saturated. Two local examples that commonly change pricing in Duncan: (1) homes built before 1981 frequently have original perimeter drains that are 60+ years old and completely failed, pushing homeowners from “interior only” budgets toward the $7,000–$18,000 exterior band when a true perimeter fix is required; and (2) when a basement has recurring dampness near a specific crack line, crack injection alone (often in the $250–$800 range for repair) may reduce leaks, but a full exterior or interior drainage plan may still be needed if water is bypassing around the crack during heavy storms.

Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, some waterproofing-related work requires a permit—especially when you’re altering foundation elements, structural components, or lot drainage patterns. Foundation excavation (especially when it changes how the foundation is exposed or affects drainage routes), structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage commonly fall under permit requirements. If a sump pump discharge line connects to a storm or sanitary system, municipal approval is typically required, since that affects existing servicing infrastructure. For structural crack repair—like horizontal cracks in block walls or any signs of structural movement—a structural engineer’s assessment is often necessary to determine whether underpinning or other structural work is needed. Your contractor should be able to coordinate engineering support for structural repairs and provide documentation that shows how the plan addresses structural stability, not just surface sealing.

To verify a contractor in Duncan before work starts, follow a simple checklist. First, confirm the right licence/registration through the Government of British Columbia licensing/registry resources (search the contractor’s legal name). Second, request a certificate of liability insurance that covers the specific work (ask for the effective dates). Third, confirm WSIB/WCB coverage—ask for the clearance letter or proof of account status, depending on the type of work and company category. If they can’t provide documentation promptly, that’s a red flag.

Next, ask whether the permit pull is included in the quote and which party submits it. A competent contractor will tell you exactly what will be permitted, what won’t, and who is responsible for approvals before excavation begins.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Duncan need?

The fundamental difference is source versus management. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, new membrane, new perimeter drainage tile, and backfill—targets water before it reaches your foundation wall. It costs significantly more and requires landscaping disruption, but when it’s done correctly, it’s the most complete way to reduce hydrostatic pressure against the wall. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—handles water after it enters. It’s usually less invasive and can be faster for homeowners who need results in a confined footprint, but it does not fully stop water pressure from building against the foundation wall itself.

In Duncan, that choice depends on how your home is performing during storms and what your foundation is made from. Poured concrete walls typically respond well to crack injection combined with interior drainage when cracks are the dominant pathway. Block foundations often have more complex moisture pathways through joints and small voids, and many homeowners still benefit from interior drainage as a practical complement to any targeted crack repair. Because Vancouver Island and the Coast experience heavy rainfall with prolonged wet backfill, sump systems often need to be sized for sustained run times, not just short bursts. That’s why backup systems matter—spring flooding and storm-related power disruptions can turn a “working” sump into a liability if the pump fails during an outage.

For cost justification, consider this: if your basement is repeatedly damp at the same exterior wall line and the perimeter drain has failed, an exterior approach in the $7,000–$18,000 band can be justified. If, however, dampness is limited and you have manageable drainage, interior waterproofing in the $3,000–$10,000 band plus crack injection can be a more budget-aligned way to protect finishes and reduce recurrence without full yard disturbance.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Duncan

Start by verifying credentials the same way you’d vet any tradesperson, but be specific about waterproofing. In British Columbia, ask for proof of the correct licence/registration for the contractor’s scope, a current certificate of liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB clearance or proof of coverage. Check the licence/registry online using the contractor’s legal name, and confirm the insurance dates match your project timeline. If they won’t show paperwork, don’t move forward—waterproofing is a system, not a patch job.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes, not lump sums. You want a breakdown that separates labour from materials (membrane, drainage tile, sump components, backfill, discharge piping) and explains what’s included for site protection and disposal. Carefully read exclusions: Are permits included? Is excavation limited to certain areas? Is landscaping restoration part of the scope or “allowance only”? For basements that show mould or salts, confirm whether remediation steps are included before any sealing product is installed.

Warranty matters. Look for a workmanship warranty (length and what’s covered), plus the product/manufacturer warranty details. Ask whether the warranty is transferable to future owners—some homeowners plan to sell soon after repairs. For payments, never pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until key milestones are complete and you’ve received documentation. Finally, insist on a written start date and completion estimate, including weather-sensitive allowances for excavation on Vancouver Island.

What is a weeping tile and does my Duncan home have one?

A weeping tile (or perimeter drainage tile) is a sloped drainage system installed around the foundation to collect groundwater and seepage, then route it to a discharge point—often historically it was connected to a sump or daylight outlet. In many older Duncan homes (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census notes 61.3% built before 1981), original weeping tile may still be present but frequently fails from age and corrosion, leaving water no place to go. You might not “see” it unless a contractor exposes the perimeter during excavation, or you locate signs like recurring dampness along specific wall lines. If you’re unsure, ask what inspection steps they use—camera inspection during excavation, targeted probing, or checking historical build details—before selecting interior-only versus exterior-source repairs.

What is the difference between waterproofing and damp-proofing?

In everyday terms, waterproofing is intended to stop or control water entry and manage hydrostatic pressure—typically through systems like exterior membranes, perimeter drainage tile, interior drain channels, and sump pump setups. Damp-proofing is more about reducing moisture transmission and staining, often relying on coatings without fully addressing water pressure or drainage. In Duncan, because heavy coastal rainfall and higher groundwater can keep backfill saturated, damp-proofing alone can miss the root cause—especially if older weeping tile has failed or if cracks provide a leakage pathway. That’s why homeowners sometimes see “surface improvements” fail during the next storm season. When you get a quote, ask which standard they’re using and whether the solution includes drainage routing and a plan for crack seepage, not just a wall coating.
