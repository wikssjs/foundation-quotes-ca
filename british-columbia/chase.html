Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit. If the work involves addressing water entry through the foundation envelope—especially where you’re modifying drainage around a footing—don’t assume it’s “just repairs.” For sump pump installations, any connection to municipal storm or sanitary services generally needs municipal approval before installation and discharge routing. Where structural crack repair is required—such as horizontal cracking in block walls or other signs of potential movement—an engineer’s assessment is often needed to confirm whether underpinning or additional structural work is required before waterproofing proceeds.

For homeowners in Chase, a good contractor should help you confirm permit triggers early. A practical approach is to ask the contractor to list the exact tasks they plan to do (excavate, install perimeter drainage, crack inject, cut and patch floors, add sump, change discharge location) and then state which items require permits. Many “non-structural, localized” steps—like targeted crack injection for small, stable leaks or replacing a window well drain where grade stays the same—may not require engineering, but the safest route is to confirm in writing based on your foundation and leak type.

To verify a contractor’s British Columbia standing, you can check: (1) the online registry for the company/person listed for the work they’re claiming; (2) a certificate of liability insurance naming you as an additional insured if possible; and (3) confirmation of WSIB/WCB coverage (or clearance letter, where applicable). Require copies before work starts and keep them with your quote package.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Chase need?

The fundamental difference is what each approach does to water. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, membrane restoration, new drainage tile, and backfill—addresses the source of water entry by preventing water from reaching the foundation. It costs more and typically causes the most landscape disruption, but on the right sites it offers the strongest long-term control because it reduces hydrostatic pressure at the wall. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—manages water after it enters and lowers the basement water level. It’s less invasive and often the best first step when access is tight, but it doesn’t stop hydrostatic pressure against the wall itself, which means long-term results depend heavily on controlling water and managing cracks.

In Chase’s Thompson–Okanagan conditions, we often see exterior work justify itself when soil is retaining moisture (clay pockets or dense silts) or when older weeping tile has failed. Poured concrete walls often respond well to crack injection when paired with perimeter drainage, because the crack can be stabilized and water can be managed. Block foundations frequently benefit from interior drainage as a practical complement—especially when exterior excavation is constrained—because water pathways can reappear through multiple small voids and cold joints.

Power reliability also matters. Spring flooding risk in BC isn’t only about volume—it’s about timing. A sump pump without backup can leave you exposed if there’s an outage right when water levels spike. The difference between options is easiest to see: upgrading to a full exterior system is commonly in the $18,000–$40,000 range, while interior drainage plus a properly designed sump system often fits the $9,000–$22,000 band; if your foundation is leaking due to failed exterior drainage, the interior-only approach can mean repeated follow-ups.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Chase

Choosing the right waterproofing contractor in Chase starts with proof you’re protected. In British Columbia, verify licensing status for the work they’re describing (check the online registry relevant to their trade and the name they’ll invoice under). Ask for a current certificate of liability insurance and confirm WSIB/WCB coverage; most reputable firms can provide a certificate and clearance/verification documents on request. If they can’t clearly show this paperwork, treat it as a red flag.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes—break the job into labour and materials, with line items for excavation or floor cutting, membrane/drainage components, discharge routing, crack injection materials, and reinstatement. Don’t accept vague “waterproofing by sealing” language; the scope should state what’s being installed and where. Confirm whether permits are included or excluded, whether disposal is included, and what the contractor does if they find additional failed tile runs or unexpected footing conditions.

Warranty matters. Ask for the workmanship warranty length and whether the product/manufacturer warranty is tied to the installed system (and whether it transfers if you sell). For payments, never provide more than about 10–15% upfront; use a holdback until substantial completion and demonstrated performance. Finally, require a start date and completion timeline in writing.

Freeze-thaw then widens cracks over time, turning minor seepage into active leakage. What is a battery backup sump pump and do I need one in Chase?

A battery backup sump pump is a secondary power system that runs the pump during a power outage, helping prevent basement flooding when water levels rise. In Chase, we see spring melt conditions where water input increases quickly; if power is interrupted during a heavy rain or storm, the risk isn’t just the water volume—it’s the timing. Many homeowners assume the main sump pump is enough, but without backup you can lose pumping at the exact moment the basement starts collecting water. Whether you “need” it depends on your leak pattern and how critical the basement is (finished space, stored items, history of flooding). If you’re budgeting, sump pump installation with backup often fits the $2,500–$5,500 band, and it’s commonly recommended when there’s recurring seepage or you’ve had near-miss outages.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — which is better?

“Better” depends on the cause of water entry and what your site can realistically support. Exterior waterproofing is generally the strongest long-term solution because it reduces hydrostatic pressure at the wall—full excavation, membrane, and drainage tile. That’s why it often makes sense on sites with clay-retaining soils or failed weeping tile and where access is workable, even though it’s usually the higher investment (commonly $18,000–$40,000). Interior waterproofing is typically less disruptive and still effective: a perimeter drain channel and sump manage water after it enters, often fitting $9,000–$22,000. For Chase homes with poured concrete, crack injection can pair well with interior drainage; for block foundations, interior drainage is often a practical complement. If you only want the least disruption, interior may be appropriate—but if the source is active, exterior control usually performs best.
