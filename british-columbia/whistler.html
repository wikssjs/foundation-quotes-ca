Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation excavation and structural crack repair typically require a building permit, particularly when the work may affect structural elements or requires changes that go beyond simple surface patching. Sump pump installations that discharge to a municipal system (storm or sanitary) generally need municipal approval, so a contractor should be able to confirm the required approvals and handle the process where applicable.

For structural crack repair—commonly horizontal cracks in block walls or any major step cracks—a structural engineer’s assessment is often necessary to determine whether underpinning or other structural measures are required. Before you sign a contract, ask whether the contractor’s scope includes engineering support for structural repairs and whether they carry the appropriate liability coverage and WSIB/WCB coverage (as applicable). Whistler projects also frequently involve lot drainage adjustments and excavation near existing hardscaping, so permitting can be the difference between a straightforward retrofit and a delayed jobsite.

To verify a contractor in Whistler, homeowners should: (1) confirm the contractor’s licence details through the appropriate online registry listing for BC; (2) request a certificate of insurance and confirm coverage amounts and that it lists you/your property as required by your agreement; (3) ask for WSIB/WCB clearance documents (or the contractor’s coverage status) before work begins; and (4) ensure the written quote lists what permits are included, who pulls them, and whether engineering is required.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Whistler need?

The fundamental difference is source control versus water management. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, new membrane, new perimeter drainage tile, and properly graded backfill—addresses where water enters and reduces hydrostatic pressure against the wall. It’s the most permanent solution, but it costs more and requires substantial landscape disruption. Interior waterproofing—typically a perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—captures water after it enters and keeps basements drier. It’s less invasive, but it does not stop water pressure on the exterior wall; it relies on consistent drainage capacity and reliable pump operation.

In Whistler’s wet, mild winters and Lower Mainland–Southwest rainfall patterns, interior solutions can be a smart choice when access is limited or when the foundation material makes exterior work complicated. Poured concrete walls often seal well with crack injection (especially when cracks are cleanly identified), while block foundations commonly benefit from interior drainage as a practical complement because block systems can allow moisture migration through joints and surface pores. If you’re dealing with ongoing seepage during prolonged storms, pairing interior drainage with sump pump backup becomes important; in British Columbia, power outages during extreme weather can create a failure point if the system only has primary power.

A common justification for paying more for exterior work is when the perimeter drainage system is fundamentally failing and water is being kept up against the foundation for weeks at a time. In those cases, an interior system in the $8,000–$18,000 band may still be sensible as an interim step, but full exterior work in the $15,000–$30,000 range can be the better long-term risk reduction—particularly for properties with repeated leaks after heavy rain events.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Whistler

Choosing a contractor in Whistler starts with verification. In British Columbia, ask for proof of licence details (as applicable to their work category), a certificate of liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB coverage status before any excavation begins. You should request the documents directly, confirm they are current, and ensure the certificate aligns with the legal name on your contract. Then, verify clearance or coverage status where applicable through the contractor’s provided documentation—don’t rely on verbal assurances.

Next, get 2–3 written, itemised quotes. You want labour and materials broken out (excavation, drainage tile, membrane, pipe/joint materials, disposal, interior drains, pump and backup system). A quote that only provides a lump sum without describing what is included makes it difficult to compare and can lead to change orders once the contractor sees actual site conditions.

Read the scope carefully: is permit pulling included or excluded, is soil hauling/disposal part of the price, and what is excluded (for example, restoring finished landscaping, decks, or driveways)? Warranty matters too—ask for the workmanship warranty length, product/manufacturer warranty terms, and whether the warranty is transferable to future homeowners if you sell.

For payment, keep it conservative: never pay more than 10–15% upfront and use a holdback until the job is substantially complete. Get a written start date and completion timeline, and ensure any seasonal access constraints (common in Whistler) are acknowledged in the schedule.

  • Ask how they identify the water source (visual tracing, moisture mapping, crack survey, drain tie-in review).
  • Confirm whether they recommend interior, exterior, or a hybrid system and why—based on observed conditions.
  • Request a detailed excavation plan (limits of work, haul route assumptions, and restoration approach).
  • Verify the pump design includes a check valve and correct discharge routing.
  • Confirm sump backup strategy (battery or water-powered) and what triggers it.
  • Check that foundation crack injection uses the correct product for crack behaviour (epoxy vs polyurethane).
  • Ask how they treat mould or efflorescence before sealing.
  • Ensure they specify pipe size, bedding, slope, and drain rock specification.

  • Confirm disposal and tipping fees are included for exterior excavation.

  • Get warranty details in writing and ask who provides it (contractor vs manufacturer).

Red flags in Whistler: vague scopes (“waterproofing as required” with no materials list), no mention of weeping tile tie-in/discharge details, missing insurance/coverage documents, refusing to provide itemised pricing or permitting responsibilities, and warranties that only cover labour with no written workmanship terms or product exclusions.

Frequently asked questions — waterproofing in Whistler

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — which is better?

In Whistler, exterior waterproofing is generally the “best” option when your leaks are driven by perimeter groundwater pressure and a failed drainage setup. Exterior excavation plus new membrane and drainage tile targets the source; interior systems typically manage water after it enters. That’s why a full exterior project usually sits in the higher range—often $15,000–$30,000—while interior perimeter drainage and sump systems commonly fall around $8,000–$18,000. That said, exterior isn’t always practical on tight lots or when landscaping/hardscaping is extensive. Many Whistler homes use a hybrid approach: crack injection where appropriate, plus interior drainage to reduce risk during storms while exterior work is planned or scoped.

Why is my basement leaking in Whistler?

Basement leaks in Whistler are commonly caused by sustained rainfall loading the backfill and keeping water under hydrostatic pressure against foundation walls and slabs. When older perimeter drain/weep systems are clogged, disconnected, or undersized, water can build up and find pathways through wall-floor transitions, joints, and cracks. Freeze-thaw cycles can also widen existing defects, making seepage more obvious over time. If your home has signs like efflorescence or recurring dampness near corners, it often indicates long-term moisture movement rather than a one-time overflow. In homes built prior to 1981, the original waterproofing and drainage components are more likely to have reached the end of their service life (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), which increases the odds you’ll need either drainage replacement or an interior retrofit.

How do I know if a foundation crack is serious?

Not all cracks are equal in British Columbia. Hairline, vertical shrinkage cracks are often less concerning, but serious indicators include horizontal cracks in block walls, step cracks, cracks that widen seasonally, and signs of water movement through the crack (wetness, seepage staining, or mineral buildup). If you notice bowing, doors/windows that suddenly misalign, or cracking that clearly progresses after heavy rain, treat it as higher risk. For structural crack repair—especially major horizontal or step cracking—an engineer assessment is often required to decide whether stabilization or underpinning is needed. For non-structural sealing, many homeowners can pursue crack injection (commonly budgeted in the $500–$2,000 band for targeted repairs), but only after confirming crack behaviour and water presence.

How much does foundation crack repair cost in Whistler?

In Whistler, foundation crack repair cost depends on the crack type, length, and whether it’s actively leaking. Targeted injection for a limited section is often budgeted in the $500–$2,000 range, assuming the crack is accessible and the scope stays limited to sealing. If multiple cracks are involved, if injection requires extensive surface prep, or if a structural engineer assessment is needed, costs can rise beyond that band. Another factor is the source water: if your perimeter drainage is failing and hydrostatic pressure is ongoing, sealing alone may not hold long-term. In those cases, contractors commonly recommend pairing crack injection with drainage improvements, which can shift the overall waterproofing project into interior ($8,000–$18,000) or exterior ($15,000–$30,000) territory depending on the solution.

Do I need a sump pump in Whistler?

A sump pump is commonly recommended in Whistler when interior drainage collects meaningful groundwater and you can’t rely on gravity discharge. Given Lower Mainland–Southwest conditions—high groundwater pressure and prolonged wet periods—a sump reduces the chance of interior flooding. If you already have frequent water in the pit area, the system may be undersized or missing backup power. Many contractors also recommend battery backup or water-powered backup because spring storms and extreme weather can cause outages; your basement shouldn’t become the “backup storage.” Pump installation budgets often land in the $1,000–$5,000 range depending on whether you’re adding a primary unit only or including backup and proper discharge routing. If you can provide photos of the sump/grade area and describe when water appears, a contractor can tell you whether sump + backup is essential or optional for your setup.

How does Whistler's soil affect my foundation?

Whistler’s foundation risk isn’t just “soil type”—it’s how soil holds water and how it responds to freeze-thaw. In coastal BC conditions, saturation and drainage limitations can keep soils loaded behind foundation walls for long periods after heavy rain. If you have clay-rich or compacted soils, moisture can linger and can increase pressure at the wall when drainage fails. Freeze-thaw then widens existing joints and cracks, giving water new pathways and accelerating deterioration. If your home’s weeping tile is old or blocked, even well-graded yards can’t prevent backfill saturation. As a result, your foundation may show seepage patterns near joints and low points rather than one-time leaks. Matching the waterproofing approach to the observed water behaviour—often via interior drains, exterior drainage replacement, or both—is the practical way to address it.
