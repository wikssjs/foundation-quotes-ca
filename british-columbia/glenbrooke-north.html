Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation-related work can require a building permit depending on what you’re changing. In general, foundation excavation around the perimeter, structural crack repair where a wall’s integrity may be impacted, and changes that affect lot drainage typically require a permit. Sump pump installations that connect discharge to municipal storm or sanitary services usually need municipal approval as well. If you’re dealing with major horizontal cracks in block walls, step cracks, or signs suggesting movement, a structural engineer’s assessment is commonly required to determine whether underpinning or other structural measures are needed before waterproofing proceeds.

What commonly requires a permit: perimeter excavation for exterior membrane/drainage replacement, work that alters foundation elements or routes, and structural crack repairs that go beyond cosmetic sealing. What often does not require a permit: interior finish removal, replacing a subpanel cover plate, basic cleanup and dehumidification, or localized crack injection where it’s strictly non-structural and does not alter load paths (still confirm with your contractor and municipality).

To verify a contractor in Glenbrooke North, start by checking their licensing status and whether they have engineering support available for structural situations. Ask for a Certificate of Insurance (liability coverage) naming you as the “owner” or showing you’re covered as required by your contract. Confirm they carry WSIB/WCB clearance for workers who will be on site. When structural review is part of the scope, request their process for coordinating engineering and written reports—then keep copies with your contract package.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Glenbrooke North need?

For Glenbrooke North homes, the key difference is what each approach does to the water problem. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, new membrane, new perimeter drainage tile, then proper backfill—addresses the source of water entry. It can be more expensive and disruptive because it requires access around the foundation, often involving excavation through landscaped areas, shrubs, and sometimes portions of driveway edges. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, sump pump—manages water after it enters. It’s less invasive, but it does not remove hydrostatic pressure acting on the wall itself.

In the Lower Mainland–Southwest, persistent rainfall and higher groundwater patterns mean interior systems frequently work best as part of a complete interception plan: a correctly sloped interior drain channel, an adequately sized sump, and reliable discharge routing away from the foundation. Poured concrete walls usually respond well to crack injection when cracks are stable—epoxy for non-moving cracks—while block foundations often benefit from interior drainage as a practical complement due to how moisture can travel through joints and surface irregularities.

Sump pump backup matters in British Columbia because wet-season events can coincide with power disruptions, and spring flooding seasons increase run time and cycling. A primary pump plus battery backup helps reduce the chance of backup failure when water overwhelms the system.

Here’s where cost differences can be justified: if your perimeter drain is original and failing and your symptoms appear after heavy rain, an exterior system in the $15,000–$30,000 range can be the more permanent answer. But if you’re seeing localized seepage at corners or along a joint and access for excavation is limited, an interior perimeter drain plus sump at roughly $8,000–$18,000 may be the smarter, less disruptive first step—especially when paired with targeted crack injection where appropriate.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Glenbrooke North

Choosing a waterproofing contractor in Glenbrooke North, British Columbia should start with verification, not marketing. Confirm they have the right licensing for the work they’re doing and ask for a Certificate of Insurance for liability coverage. You want to see the coverage active for your job, with an owner’s name listed if required by your contract. Next, ask about WSIB/WCB coverage for their workers; the easiest approach is to request clearance documentation before work begins and verify it matches the company that will be on site.

Then request 2–3 itemized written quotes—broken down by labour and materials, not just a lump sum. A good quote will show what’s included for excavation (if exterior), membrane type, drainage tile size, filter fabric, backfill and compaction steps, and disposal/restoration allowances. Pay attention to what’s excluded: for example, whether permit fees are included, whether engineering is required for structural cracks, and whether landscaping restoration or concrete patching is included or billed separately. Ask how they’ll handle mould or efflorescence remediation before any sealing products go on.

Warranty should be specific: workmanship warranty length, product/manufacturer warranty details, and whether it’s transferable to the next homeowner. For payment, never agree to more than 10–15% upfront; use a holdback and release payments only after key milestones are complete. Finally, insist on a start date and completion estimate in writing, including weather-related contingencies—especially important in a region with prolonged rainy periods.

Soil in the Lower Mainland–Southwest tends to create problems through persistent saturation and drainage challenges rather than extreme seasonal swelling. Practically, this can push costs toward more comprehensive solutions like interior drainage systems (often $8,000–$18,000) or, when exterior access is workable and original perimeter drainage is failing, exterior excavation and drainage replacement (often $15,000–$30,000).

How long does waterproofing last?

Waterproofing longevity depends on whether the system addresses the source of water entry or only manages water after it enters. Exterior waterproofing—when properly detailed with membrane, drainage tile, correct slopes, and proper backfill—can last many years, especially in wet Lower Mainland conditions where long-term drainage performance is critical. Interior systems are also effective, but they can be more sensitive to water volume and pump reliability; if power fails, backup planning becomes important. Targeting stable cracks with the right injection method can also improve service life, but moving cracks typically require deeper investigation. For homeowners budgeting in Glenbrooke North, it’s common to compare exterior systems ($15,000–$30,000) against interior approaches ($8,000–$18,000) because the more permanent option usually costs more—but can reduce the likelihood of recurring moisture events.

Can I waterproof my basement from the inside only?

Yes, you can often waterproof from the inside only in Glenbrooke North, and it’s a common option when excavation access is limited by landscaping, driveways, or tight urban lot setbacks. Interior perimeter drainage channels and a sump system can intercept seepage after it enters, reducing floor wetness and lowering humidity-related mould risk. However, interior-only work generally doesn’t remove the hydrostatic pressure acting on the wall itself, so results depend heavily on sump performance, pump backup, discharge routing, and how much water is entering. If your main issue is roof runoff, re-grading and downspout extensions can help (commonly $2,500–$8,000). If your perimeter drainage has failed and water is entering consistently after rain, you may eventually need exterior excavation for the most complete solution.

What causes foundation cracks in Glenbrooke North?

Foundation cracks in Glenbrooke North are typically caused by a combination of moisture movement and temperature changes. Heavy rain can saturate backfill, and freeze-thaw cycles in British Columbia can widen joints and micro-cracks. If drainage has failed—such as older weeping tile systems—the foundation may experience persistent wet pressure, which can accelerate cracking over time. In addition, if the soil near the foundation has poor drainage, repeated wetting can lead to small movements and settlement that show up as hairline cracks or, in more concerning cases, step or horizontal cracks. The right fix depends on crack type: hairline, stable cracks may be suited to injection (often in the $500–$2,000 band), while structural or actively leaking cracks may require engineering review and a different injection system.
