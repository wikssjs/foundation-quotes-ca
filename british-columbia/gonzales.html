Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit. If you’re modifying how water leaves the property—for example, connecting a sump pump discharge line to storm or sanitary sewer systems—municipal approval is usually required before any tie-in. For structural crack repair (especially horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracks, or signs of foundation movement), a structural engineer’s assessment is often required to determine whether underpinning or other structural work is needed.

When you hire a contractor in Gonzales, ask whether they have engineering support for structural repairs, and verify they carry liability insurance and valid worker protection coverage (WSIB/WCB) for their employees and subcontractors. In practice, this matters because waterproofing and drainage are not only “water control”—they can involve structural implications, excavation near foundations, and permitted plumbing or drainage routing.

Step-by-step checks you can do as a homeowner:

  • Confirm contractor credentials: look for the correct B.C. licence/registration on the provincial registry (and ensure it matches the scope).
  • Request a certificate of insurance and confirm liability coverage is current and includes jobsite work.
  • Ask for proof of WSIB/WCB clearance (or coverage) for the workers performing the excavation and interior concrete work.
  • For structural repairs, request the engineer’s assessment plan and letter scope before injection or sealing begins.
  • Ask whether the contractor will pull the permit (and whether it’s included in the quote).

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Gonzales need?

The key difference is that exterior waterproofing is source control: full excavation along the foundation, new membrane, new drainage tile, and properly engineered backfill, which reduces how much water reaches the foundation wall in the first place. Interior waterproofing is symptom control: it collects water after entry using a perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and a sump pump (with backup if needed). Because the Lower Mainland–Southwest often has high groundwater and prolonged saturation, interior drainage can work well for many basements, but it doesn’t eliminate hydrostatic pressure on the wall itself. That’s why the best solution depends on where water is entering and how your foundation material behaves.

In Gonzales, poured concrete walls often respond well to crack injection when cracks are the primary leak path, because the concrete can be sealed to limit seepage. Block foundations, however, frequently require interior drainage as a practical complement: mortar joints and block movement can allow water pathways that are hard to fully eliminate from the inside. Sump pump backup is worth discussing in B.C. spring flooding periods and during outage-prone wet spells—backup reduces the chance that collected groundwater overtops the system when power drops.

Here’s when the price difference is justified: if you’re seeing recurring wetting along multiple wall sections after heavy rain, an exterior system budget of $15,000–$30,000 can be worth it because it rebuilds the drainage and waterproofing envelope at the source. If you have a single problem area and the rest of the foundation shows limited moisture, an interior approach in the $8,000–$18,000 range may deliver a faster, less disruptive improvement without unnecessary landscaping removal.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Gonzales

Choosing a waterproofing contractor in Gonzales starts with verifying credentials the right way. Then, compare apples to apples: ask for 2–3 itemised written quotes with a labour and materials breakdown (not just a lump sum). On payment schedule, a safe practice is never paying more than 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until the job is complete and defects are corrected. Finally, get the start date and completion estimate in writing so you’re not left waiting during rainy stretches when soils stay saturated.

What is the difference between waterproofing and damp-proofing?

In Gonzales and across British Columbia’s Lower Mainland–Southwest, the term “damp-proofing” usually means reducing minor moisture at the surface—think coating or surface sealing meant to slow moisture transfer. True waterproofing addresses water under pressure, especially when groundwater and hydrostatic pressure push against basement walls and slab edges after prolonged rain. Practically, waterproofing plans often include a drainage system (exterior tile and membrane, or an interior perimeter drain with a sump) plus sealing of the specific leakage pathways (like crack injection). If you’re paying for interior drainage, you’re typically managing water after it enters; if you’re paying for full exterior excavation and membrane with new drainage tile, you’re targeting the source. In our region, due to persistent saturation, “waterproofing” should be designed around drainage and pressure control, not just coatings.

What is a battery backup sump pump and do I need one in Gonzales?

A battery backup sump pump is designed to keep pumping if the main power fails—so collected groundwater doesn’t rise into your basement during an outage. In Gonzales and across British Columbia’s Lower Mainland–Southwest, power can be interrupted during storms and wet spring periods, when heavy rainfall and saturated soils increase water collection demand. A system with battery backup typically provides short-to-intermediate run time while the grid is down. Whether you “need” one depends on your basement risk profile: the size/position of the sump, how quickly water accumulates, and whether you’ve had prior outages coinciding with wet weather. If your basement has a history of water reaching the floor after storms, backup is often a smart risk-reducer. If you’re budgeting, sump pump installs with backup can fall in the $1,000–$5,000 range, depending on system design and components.
