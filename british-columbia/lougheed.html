Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, many foundation-related changes typically require a building permit, particularly where you’re excavating around a foundation, performing structural crack repair, or altering how water is managed off the lot. As a homeowner in Lougheed, you should expect permits are commonly required for exterior excavation work, changes to drainage routing, and any repair that impacts structural components.

Sump pump installations can also require municipal approval when the discharge ties into storm or sanitary services, or when the discharge route changes how water leaves the property. For structural crack repair—especially horizontal cracks in block foundations, major step cracks, or any indication of movement—an engineer’s assessment is often required to confirm whether the crack is purely water ingress or whether underpinning/structural work is needed.

Step-by-step, here’s how to verify a contractor before you sign in Lougheed:

  • Ask for the contractor’s BC licence/registration details (and confirm the trade scope matches your project).
  • Request a current certificate of insurance for liability (make sure it lists your property address if feasible, and confirm expiry date).
  • Ask for proof of WSIB/WCB coverage if applicable to the workforce and subcontractors.
  • For any structural scope, request documentation showing they can coordinate engineering support and understand permit requirements.
  • Request the permit/paperwork plan in writing: who pulls permits, what’s included, and what you must approve.
  • Verify any subcontractors are covered (insurance and WSIB/WCB documentation should be provided).

If a contractor won’t clearly explain what requires a permit and what doesn’t, treat that as a caution sign and ask for written clarification.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Lougheed need?

The fundamental difference is that exterior waterproofing targets the source—full excavation, new membrane, new perimeter drainage tile, and properly staged backfill—so water is directed away before it presses against walls. Interior waterproofing is typically a response after water enters: an interior perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump reduce wet floors and seepage, but they don’t remove hydrostatic pressure acting on the exterior wall.

In Lougheed’s Lower Mainland–Southwest climate, where persistent rainfall and high groundwater can keep basement walls under pressure for long periods, many homeowners need interior systems that can handle peaks reliably. Poured concrete foundations often seal better with crack injection when cracks are stable, because injection can fill voids and stop seepage routes effectively. Block foundations frequently have more complexity at joints and interfaces, so interior drainage often becomes the practical complement even when crack treatment is done.

Power reliability matters too. During spring flooding patterns in British Columbia, sump pumps can run continuously for stretches. That’s why backup systems are often recommended: a battery backup can keep pumping during short outages while still protecting your interior finish and preventing overflow.

Where does the price difference get justified? If you’re dealing with saturated backfill and a failed perimeter drainage system, full exterior waterproofing may be worth moving from $8,000 – $18,000 (typical interior perimeter drain and sump scope) up into the $15,000 – $30,000 band—because you’re restoring the primary water pathway, not only managing symptoms. Conversely, if you’ve identified localized interior seepage with no active wall leakage path outside, an interior French drain and properly sized sump may solve the problem without the disruption of excavation.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Lougheed

How long does waterproofing last?

The lifespan depends on the method and whether the water pathway is truly addressed. Exterior waterproofing with a properly detailed membrane and drainage tile system can last many years because it prevents water from loading the foundation. Interior drainage systems also last well, but they manage water after entry, so performance depends on sump design, discharge, and whether the perimeter is still shedding groundwater toward the drain. Crack injection can last a long time for stable, non-moving cracks, but active leaks or ongoing hydrostatic pressure may require polyurethane injection and/or drainage upgrades. In Lougheed’s Lower Mainland–Southwest climate (wet winters and freeze-thaw), regular maintenance like keeping downspouts functional and verifying sump operation can extend service life. If you’re budgeting, it helps to compare interior ranges like $8,000 – $18,000 against exterior work in $15,000 – $30,000 based on your source of water entry.

Can I waterproof my basement from the inside only?

Yes, many Lougheed homeowners do interior-only waterproofing, especially when excavation isn’t practical due to decks, landscaping, or tight side yards. Interior French drains and sump systems can be very effective at reducing seepage and keeping floors dry by collecting water after it enters. However, interior work doesn’t stop hydrostatic pressure acting on the wall itself, so it’s best when you need to manage recurring moisture rather than fully restore exterior drainage. If your foundation has stable, non-moving cracks, crack injection can complement interior drainage and reduce seepage paths. In cases where exterior drainage has failed and backfill is consistently saturated, full exterior excavation may offer the most durable “source control.” Your contractor should explain which water pathway is active before recommending interior-only work.

What causes foundation cracks in Lougheed?

In Lougheed, foundation cracks often develop from a combination of groundwater persistence, wet soil saturation, and freeze-thaw. When soil stays saturated after heavy rainfall, it can keep hydrostatic pressure against basement walls and slabs. Freeze-thaw cycles then widen existing joints and micro-cracks, allowing more water to penetrate and accelerating deterioration. While clay-rich expansive soils are more associated with pronounced seasonal swelling in parts of Canada, Lower Mainland problems often present as persistent saturation and drainage challenges rather than extreme expansion. Older housing stock may add risk because original weeping tile systems can fail over time, changing how water moves around the foundation. If cracks appear horizontal or show signs of movement, you should treat it as more than a simple leak issue and request a structural assessment.
