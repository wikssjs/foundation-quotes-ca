Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, certain foundation-related waterproofing and drainage changes typically require a building permit, especially when you’re altering foundation elements, performing structural crack repair, or changing how lot drainage is handled. Foundation excavation for exterior waterproofing, structural crack repair that affects load-bearing conditions (or suggests movement), and modifications that change discharge routes or drainage patterns commonly fall into permit-required work. In addition, sump pump installations that tie into storm or sanitary sewer systems generally need municipal approval.

For structural crack repair—particularly horizontal cracks in block walls, significant step cracking, or any cracking that raises concern about movement—a structural engineer’s assessment is often required to determine whether underpinning or other structural measures are needed. As a homeowner, ask whether the contractor has engineering support for structural repairs, plus liability insurance and proper worker coverage (WSIB/WCB) before work begins.

How to verify before you sign: (1) ask for their BC licence/registration information and confirm it on the relevant online provincial registry; (2) request a current certificate of insurance and check expiry dates and coverage limits; (3) ask for proof of WSIB/WCB clearance or account status (or provide a clearance letter) and confirm it matches your contractor’s legal name.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Haney need?

The key difference is where the water problem is solved. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, membrane application, installation (or replacement) of drainage tile, then backfill—aims to stop water at the foundation perimeter and reduce hydrostatic pressure before it enters. It’s more expensive and disruptive because it requires excavation along the wall, disposal of excavated soil, and restoring landscaped areas. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—collects water after it penetrates and redirects it away, which usually means less demo, but it doesn’t remove the pressure on the wall itself.

In Haney and the broader Lower Mainland–Southwest, persistent rainfall and higher groundwater pressure can keep basement walls under load for long stretches. That’s why poured-concrete walls often respond well to targeted crack injection plus a functioning interior drainage system, while block foundations commonly need interior drainage as a practical complement, especially if exterior drainage details are unknown or have failed. Freeze-thaw in BC also means cracks can widen during wet/cold transitions; a passive patch can look fine in dry weeks and then fail when saturation returns.

Sump pump backup matters here because power loss during storm events can be more than a nuisance—the longer water sits, the more likely you’ll see efflorescence and ongoing seepage. A battery backup (or other approved backup) helps bridge short outages until power returns.

As a practical cost example: if your issue is an interior seepage line that can be mapped to specific wall cracks and captured with an interior perimeter drain and sump, the total is often closer to $8,000–$18,000. If the source is a failed perimeter drain and saturated backfill pushing hydrostatic pressure, exterior excavation may be justified because it targets the source, aligning with $15,000–$30,000.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Haney

Choosing the right waterproofing contractor in Haney is less about flashy marketing and more about proof: licensing/registration status where applicable, liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB coverage. Start by asking for (1) their BC contractor registration/licence information and confirming it through the online provincial registry; (2) a current certificate of insurance and verifying the legal company name matches; and (3) proof of WSIB/WCB coverage or a clearance letter so you’re not left holding the bag if a worker is injured.

Then request 2–3 itemised written quotes, not lump sums. A solid quote breaks labour and materials into categories like excavation (if exterior), membrane/drainage materials, sump pit and pump selection (including backup), crack injection materials, disposal, and restoration allowances. Make sure the scope clearly states what’s included and what’s excluded—common exclusions include hauling/disposal, permits, engineered sign-offs for structural concerns, and restoration beyond basic grading.

Warranty should be specific: ask for the workmanship warranty length and whether it covers labour plus callbacks. Also ask about product/manufacturer warranties, and whether the warranty is transferable if you sell the home. For payment, keep it conservative—never more than 10–15% upfront, and use a holdback until the waterproofing system is complete and tested. Finally, insist on a written timeline with a start date and completion estimate, and clarify weather-related scheduling for exterior excavation in BC’s wet season.

What is a battery backup sump pump and do I need one in Haney?

A battery backup sump pump is an additional power system that keeps pumping if the main electrical supply fails. In British Columbia, heavy rain periods and spring high-water seasons can create conditions where outages happen, and even short interruptions matter because collected water can rise quickly in a basement. If your home relies on a sump to prevent water from backing up, backup protection is often worth considering—especially when your interior drainage system is handling active seepage. Whether you “need” it depends on your basement layout, how quickly water enters during storms, and whether you have had prior outage-related wetting. A battery backup upgrade typically falls within the broader sump upgrade pricing band of $1,000–$5,000, depending on the system choice and installation requirements.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — which is better?

Neither option is universally “better”—the right method depends on what’s driving the water. Exterior waterproofing is the source solution: excavation, membrane, and new drainage tile reduce hydrostatic pressure before water reaches the foundation. It’s more disruptive, so it’s generally recommended when groundwater pressure is the main issue or when original perimeter drainage has failed. Interior waterproofing (perimeter drain channel, sump pit, sump pump) is more targeted to managing water after entry. In Haney’s coastal climate and Lower Mainland–Southwest market, interior solutions can work well when combined with crack sealing and when exterior access is difficult, but they don’t remove the underlying pressure on the wall. As a rough guidance, if you’re choosing between $8,000–$18,000 (interior) and $15,000–$30,000 (exterior), the higher cost is typically justified when seepage is recurring across multiple wall segments or the perimeter drainage system is clearly failing.

Why is my basement leaking in Haney?

In Haney and the wider Lower Mainland–Southwest region, basement leaks are commonly driven by saturation and groundwater pressure. When prolonged rainfall keeps backfill saturated, water can push against basement walls and slab edges, especially if the original weeping tile system is undersized, clogged, or has aged. Freeze-thaw cycles can widen existing cracks and joints, creating fresh pathways that weren’t as noticeable during drier periods. Another frequent cause is surface water runoff—like downspouts directing water toward the foundation—which can compound problems when drainage can’t keep up. If you see dampness near corners, along wall-slab joints, or around window wells, the leak pathway is often different from what you’d expect, which is why a basic site assessment and crack mapping matters before choosing injection or drain work.

How do I know if a foundation crack is serious?

Crack seriousness depends on crack type, location, and whether the crack is active (moving or actively leaking). Hairline, vertical cracks can sometimes be non-structural, especially in poured concrete, and may be addressed with targeted crack sealing after mapping. Structural concern increases with horizontal cracks in block walls, step cracking patterns, cracks that show offset/displacement, or cracks that expand during wet seasons. In British Columbia, freeze-thaw can make active leaks look “sudden” even if the pathway existed for longer. You can also watch for signs like persistent seepage, efflorescence (white salt deposits), worsening dampness after heavy rain, or water pooling near the crack line. For any major or shifting crack, ask for an engineering assessment before sealing—especially because the fix may require more than injection alone. Foundation crack injection is often priced around $500–$2,000, but structural cracks may require additional assessment and scope.
