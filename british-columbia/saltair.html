Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit. If you’re installing or modifying drainage that connects to municipal systems, sump pump connections to storm or sanitary sewer require municipal approval. For structural crack repair—especially horizontal cracks in block walls or major step cracks—a structural engineer’s assessment is often required to confirm whether underpinning or other structural work is needed. When you’re comparing bids in Saltair, ask whether the contractor plans for engineering support and permits as part of their scope for the specific crack conditions they expect to find.

Step-by-step verification a homeowner can do: (1) Confirm the contractor is licensed/qualified for the type of work they’re advertising (for example, general contracting and any specialty trade credentials where applicable). Start with the relevant provincial online registry and then match the company name exactly to the quote and invoice. (2) Request a certificate of insurance showing liability coverage; verify it’s active for the project period. (3) Ask for proof of WSIB/WCB coverage and look for the clearance letter or account confirmation referenced by the certificate. (4) For jobs that likely trigger engineering, request that the contractor name the engineer support process in writing and include how their engineering plan ties to the permit package.

Finally, ensure the contract clarifies who pulls permits and who pays fees—so “hidden” regulatory work doesn’t appear after you sign.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Saltair need?

In Saltair, the core difference is whether you address the water source or manage water after it enters. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, new membrane, and replacement perimeter drainage tile—targets the entry point and reduces hydrostatic pressure at the wall. It’s the most permanent approach, but it’s also the most disruptive because yards must be opened, backfilled, and restored. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—captures seepage inside the foundation and keeps the basement drier. It’s less invasive, often avoiding major landscaping disruption, but it doesn’t stop hydrostatic pressure from reaching the wall.

Given Lower Mainland–Southwest conditions (higher groundwater and prolonged rainfall), many basements benefit from drainage-first thinking. Poured concrete walls often respond well to crack injection combined with interior drainage, because sealed cracks reduce direct pathways. Block foundations frequently need a practical complement: interior drainage and sump management paired with crack sealing, because water can travel through block joints and voids in ways that interior capture systems handle more reliably. Freeze-thaw can reopen pathways and widen joints over time, so we typically recommend pairing sealing measures with a pumping and drainage strategy—especially where original weeping tile is suspected to be undersized or failing.

Backup sump pump systems are especially relevant in BC’s wet spring cycles. When heavy runoff hits, sump pumps can run more frequently, and power interruptions—even short ones—can quickly turn into basement saturation. That’s when battery backup or a water-powered backup can protect your finished space and reduce cleanup.

Where the price difference makes sense: if inspection shows failed perimeter drainage on multiple sides, exterior work in the $15,000–$30,000 band may be justified because it reduces long-term failure risk. If leaks are localized and controlled with interior drain and sump, interior waterproofing in the $8,000–$18,000 band can be a better value—provided the scope includes proper sump sizing and discharge planning.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Saltair

Choosing the right waterproofing contractor in Saltair starts with verifying credentials and coverage, not just the price. In British Columbia, confirm the contractor is properly authorized for the work they’re proposing, then verify liability insurance by requesting a current certificate showing active coverage for your project dates. For WSIB/WCB, ask for clearance or proof of coverage documentation; it should be tied to the exact contractor/company on your quote. If structural crack repair is anticipated, ask whether they have engineering support in place and include it in the scope.

Next, require 2–3 itemised written quotes. You want a breakdown that separates labour and materials, specifies the drainage components (pipe size, sump capacity, discharge method), and states what’s included for disposal and restoration. Read the scope line-by-line: is permit pulling included (where required), is soil disposal included, and what repairs are excluded (driveway replacement, full landscape restoration, paint/drywall matching)?

Warranty should be clear and enforceable. Ask for the workmanship warranty length, whether product/manufacturer warranties apply, and if those warranties are transferable to future owners. For payment, don’t pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until the job is complete and inspected. Finally, insist on a written start date and completion estimate that matches the scope (wet-season excavation and curing times can affect timelines).

What is a battery backup sump pump and do I need one in Saltair?

A battery backup sump pump is a secondary pumping system that runs during power outages, allowing the sump to continue removing water when the primary pump loses electricity. In Saltair and coastal BC, spring storms and heavy rainfall cycles can coincide with higher pump demand, so backup protection reduces the risk of basement flooding during short outages. Whether you “need” one depends on how quickly water enters, basement finish value, and how critical the space is. If your sump runs frequently during wet periods, or you’ve already experienced power interruptions during storm weather, battery backup is often a wise safeguard. Contractors may quote sump systems within the typical band of $1,000–$5,000, with backup options typically increasing cost.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — which is better?

Neither is “better” in every case; the right choice depends on water entry and site access. Why is my basement leaking in Saltair?

Most basement leaks in Saltair come from water pressure and pathways that activate during prolonged rainfall and freeze-thaw cycles. If perimeter drainage is blocked, undersized, or failed, groundwater can build up and seep through cracks, mortar joints, or slab edges. Older homes—especially those with older drainage systems—are more likely to show recurring seepage because the original weeping tile may be ineffective after decades. Another common cause is surface water management: downspouts or grading that directs water toward the foundation can add extra load, making interior symptoms worse. If the leak worsens in wet seasons and improves slightly afterward, that pattern strongly suggests drainage capacity issues rather than isolated plumbing leaks. A proper site-focused inspection usually identifies where water enters before selecting crack injection versus a full perimeter drainage plan.
