Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, basement excavation and changes that affect drainage or potentially structural elements typically require a building permit. For Clinton-Glenwood homeowners, common permit-relevant work includes foundation excavation where you’re exposing walls, structural crack repair when the crack pattern could indicate movement (especially horizontal cracks in block walls or major step cracks), and alterations to lot drainage that change how roof and surface water is managed. In addition, if a sump pump discharge is connected to the storm system or sanitary system, you’ll generally need municipal approval before the connection is made.

For structural crack repair, a structural engineer’s assessment is often required to determine whether underpinning or other structural work is needed rather than only sealing. Before you approve a quote, confirm the contractor has engineering support for structural scope and carries liability insurance. You should also verify WSIB/WCB coverage (or the applicable coverage status for the trade) so you’re protected if something goes wrong during excavation, concrete cutting, or membrane installation.

How to verify things step-by-step: first, ask for the contractor’s business details and check their provincial registration/licence information through the relevant online registry used in British Columbia. Next, request a current certificate of insurance listing you as an additional insured where possible and confirm the coverage is active for the project dates. Finally, obtain proof of WSIB/WCB clearance/coverage (the documentation is typically issued directly to the contractor, not to the homeowner), and keep copies with your contract file.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Clinton-Glenwood need?

The fundamental difference is what the system targets. Exterior waterproofing (full excavation, new membrane, new drainage tile, and backfill) addresses the source of water entry by creating a managed drainage pathway outside the foundation. It costs more and requires major landscape disruption, but it’s the most complete way to relieve hydrostatic pressure at the wall. Interior waterproofing (perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump) manages water after it enters—by collecting it and pumping it away—so your basement stays dry, even during high groundwater and prolonged rainfall.

In Clinton-Glenwood and the broader Lower Mainland–Southwest market, elevated groundwater and persistent saturation often mean exterior solutions are prioritized when exterior access is feasible and when original perimeter drainage is clearly failing. When exterior work isn’t practical—due to hardscapes, limited access, or the foundation being difficult to excavate—interior systems become the practical path. For poured concrete walls, crack injection is frequently an effective complementary measure because the wall surface can be properly prepared and injected; for block foundations, interior drainage is often essential because block joints can channel water and the foundation may need a secondary collection strategy.

Sump pump backup systems matter because spring flooding periods can bring outages and unstable power. A battery backup (or water-powered backup where designed for the site) can prevent water from backing up into the basement during pump interruptions.

To see where the price difference is justified: if you’re facing widespread exterior saturation and a failed weeping tile line, an exterior excavation plus membrane often lands in the $15,000–$30,000 band. When the leak is mainly at a few cracks, localized corners, or window areas, a targeted strategy (for example, crack injection in the $500–$2,000 band plus an interior perimeter drain) can avoid full excavation and still protect the basement.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Clinton-Glenwood

Choosing the right waterproofing contractor in Clinton-Glenwood starts with proof, not promises. In British Columbia, verify licensing/registration appropriate for the trade and ask for their liability insurance certificate (active coverage for the project dates). For workers on site, confirm WSIB/WCB coverage status and request the clearance documentation—don’t accept verbal confirmation. A reliable contractor will provide these documents quickly and consistently.

Next, request 2–3 itemised written quotes with a labour and materials breakdown rather than a single lump-sum number. The scope should clearly state what’s included (excavation limits, disposal of excavated material, surface restoration, sump discharge routing, and whether permits are pulled). Also confirm exclusions: for example, who handles interior floor replacement if the system requires cutting, or whether mould remediation is included if efflorescence is present.

Warranty matters. Ask for the workmanship warranty length and whether it’s backed by the installer or only through a product manufacturer. Product/manufacturer warranties should specify what fails and for how long; workmanship warranties should be in writing, and you should ask whether they’re transferable if you sell the home.

Payment schedule: keep upfront deposits modest. A good rule is never paying more than 10–15% upfront, then holding back a portion until key milestones are complete and the site is left properly restored. Also insist on a written start date and an estimated completion timeline so you can plan around excavation and drying times.

  • Ask for local references from Lower Mainland–Southwest basements with similar foundation type and leak patterns.
  • Confirm they distinguish between stable cracks (epoxy) and active leaks (polyurethane) rather than “one product fits all.”
  • Ensure their sump plan includes a discharge route that won’t create new wet spots or backflow issues.
  • Verify whether battery backup is recommended based on your basement risk and power interruption history.
  • Check that permits/paperwork responsibilities are written into the contract scope.
  • Demand itemised disposal/restoration inclusions (topsoil, backfill compaction, landscaping reinstatement).
  • Confirm the quote includes a site-access plan for tight lots (protecting fences, stairs, and driveways).
  • Look for a written maintenance recommendation for the drainage system (keeping weeping tile outlets clear).
  • Ask how they will protect interior finishes during installation (masking, containment for dust/mould).
  • Verify start-to-finish sequencing (drying requirements, cure times for injections/membranes).
  • Check insurance expiry dates and WSIB/WCB documentation before signing.
  • Avoid contractors who won’t put details in writing or who won’t explain why they chose exterior vs interior.

Red flags to watch for in Clinton-Glenwood: vague scopes (“waterproofing” without details of drainage routing), quoting a full exterior solution when the problem clearly appears localized (or the opposite—offering only crack injection when multiple wall sections are failing), skipping an assessment of weeping tile condition or water table symptoms, offering “lifetime” claims without identifying product/manufacturer limits, and requesting large upfront payments well beyond 10–15% before work begins.

What is a battery backup sump pump and do I need one in Clinton-Glenwood?

A battery backup sump pump is a system that automatically runs the sump when power goes out, preventing groundwater from rising and backing into your basement during outages. In Clinton-Glenwood (and across British Columbia), spring flooding periods and heavy rain events can coincide with unstable power conditions, and that’s where backup protection becomes valuable. If your interior waterproofing plan includes a sump, it’s common to budget for backup within the $1,000–$5,000 range depending on the battery configuration and installation complexity.
