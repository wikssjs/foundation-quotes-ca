Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, certain foundation and drainage changes typically require a permit. In general, foundation excavation, structural crack repair (especially where there are horizontal or step-pattern crack concerns), and changes to how lot drainage is handled often need a building permit. If your sump pump discharge connects to the storm or sanitary sewer, municipal approval is usually required before tying in the discharge route. Because rules can vary by the details of the connection and the scope of work, it’s important to confirm what applies to your exact plan before work begins.

For structural crack repair—particularly with block walls where horizontal cracking suggests possible movement—many contractors arrange for a structural engineer’s assessment to determine whether underpinning or additional structural work is needed. A reputable View Royal contractor should explain whether engineering support is included or required and should carry liability insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage where applicable for their workers.

Step-by-step, a homeowner can verify compliance: (1) ask for the contractor’s licence information and check it through the appropriate online provincial registry; (2) request a current certificate of insurance and confirm liability coverage limits match the job; (3) ask for proof of WSIB/WCB coverage (or clearance documentation) and verify the dates; (4) confirm whether permits will be pulled by the contractor or if you must manage them; and (5) get the permit number (or planned permit steps) in writing before scheduling excavation or structural repairs.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does View Royal need?

The fundamental difference is straightforward. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, new membrane, new drainage tile, and backfill—addresses the water entry point. It’s more expensive and disruptive because it requires opening up the soil along the foundation perimeter. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—manages water after it gets past the wall, improving dryness and reducing hydrostatic pressure within the basement floor system, but it does not permanently stop water from reaching the wall.

In View Royal, where heavy rainfall can saturate backfill and local groundwater can stay elevated after storms, source control often performs best when the perimeter drain/weeping tile system has failed or when you have recurring dampness along multiple wall sections. That said, exterior work isn’t always necessary. If the problem is mainly localized (for example, a damp window well area or a short crack line), interior solutions can be justified—especially where excavation would damage mature landscaping or where access is limited.

Foundation type affects the decision. Poured concrete walls often seal better with crack injection when the wall is otherwise stable, but ongoing groundwater pressure can still defeat a “seal and hope” strategy. Block foundations commonly benefit from an interior perimeter drain/sump as a practical complement because water can migrate through mortar joints and around irregularities. Given British Columbia’s occasional power disruptions during storm periods, a sump pump setup with backup (battery or water-powered depending on site design) is often a prudent protection layer, particularly if you’ve had water intrusion after heavy rain.

For example, if your home shows active seepage along a single crack line, crack injection in the $250 – $800 band may be appropriate as a targeted step—but if the perimeter drainage is failed and water is pressing against the wall, you should expect to add interior drainage work in the $3,000 – $10,000 range, or consider exterior excavation closer to $7,000 – $18,000 when source control is the real root cause.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in View Royal

Start by confirming the contractor is properly authorized for British Columbia work and protected if something goes wrong. Ask for their BC licence details (and check the registry online), request a current certificate of liability insurance, and verify their WSIB/WCB coverage or clearance letter for workers. A contractor who can’t provide documentation promptly usually signals sloppy estimating or poor site practices—both can cost you later when a leak returns.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes. You want a breakdown of labour and materials by component (excavation/membrane/drain tile, interior drain channel, pump and backup, crack injection labour and materials, disposal and restoration). Avoid quotes that hide scope in vague language like “waterproofing system” without specifying what’s being installed. Check whether permits are included, whether disposal is included, and what restoration is covered—especially if exterior excavation is part of the plan.

Warranty matters in coastal BC. Ask for the workmanship warranty length, what products are covered and whether the manufacturer warranty is direct or limited through the installer. Also confirm whether any warranty is transferable if you sell. For payment, never agree to more than 10–15% upfront; a holdback until the job is complete and tested is standard. Finally, get a written start date and a realistic completion estimate—water management work is often timed around drying and scheduling, so the timeline should be specific.

  • Provide a written scope with quantities (linear feet of drain tile, number of sump liners/pipes, crack injection count).
  • Identify the water entry likely cause (failed weeping tile, crack path, window well runoff) and how they’ll verify it.
  • Explain exterior vs interior design and what is excluded (e.g., mould remediation, grading, landscaping restoration).
  • Confirm disposal and backfill/compaction standards for excavation jobs.
  • List pump model options and whether backup is battery or water-powered (and why).
  • Include discharge routing details (sump discharge to approved destination; avoid guesswork).
  • State how and when the system will be tested (water test, pump run test, discharge verification).
  • Provide documentation for BC permits responsibility (contractor vs homeowner).
  • Give a workmanship warranty and product/manufacturer warranty in writing.
  • Use a payment schedule with a holdback until completion and commissioning.
  • Offer clear site protection plans (dust control, safe access, and protecting decks/driveways).
  • Show proof of insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage before signing anything.

Specific red flags in View Royal include: (1) contractors who promise “crack injection fixes everything” without addressing perimeter drainage; (2) skipping a moisture source diagnosis (no mention of efflorescence, drain status, or how water is moving); (3) vague warranty language such as “up to” or “subject to manufacturer terms” without a clear workmanship term; (4) requiring large upfront payments beyond 10–15%; and (5) no written scope for discharge routing, excavation restoration, or disposal—these omissions commonly lead to repeat leaks and change-order disputes.

Frequently asked questions — waterproofing in View Royal

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — which is better?

In View Royal, “better” depends on whether you need source control or water management. Exterior waterproofing (excavation, membrane, and drainage tile) is best when the perimeter drainage is failing or when you have recurring dampness along long wall runs after heavy rain. Interior waterproofing (perimeter drain channel and sump) is often the practical choice when excavation access is limited, or when you’re addressing symptoms after water enters. It’s common for exterior jobs to land in the $7,000 – $18,000 range, while interior drainage/sump work often comes in around $3,000 – $10,000. If you’re seeing active seepage after storms, ask your contractor to explain how they’ll control water movement around the foundation—not just seal one spot.

Why is my basement leaking in View Royal?

Basement leaks in British Columbia are usually driven by water movement around the foundation: heavy rainfall saturates the soil, groundwater can stay elevated longer after storms, and failed perimeter drainage lets water press against basement walls. In View Royal—especially in older homes—original weeping tile may have corroded, and landscaping grading may direct runoff toward the foundation. You may also notice efflorescence or damp patches that return after rain; that points to ongoing moisture pathways. If you have any crack, hydrostatic pressure can turn a small hairline into a leak line over time, particularly when freeze-thaw widens the path. A proper inspection typically checks drainage condition, wall elevations, and crack patterns before recommending injection or excavation.

How do I know if a foundation crack is serious?

Crack severity is less about the width you can measure today and more about crack type and whether movement appears ongoing. In View Royal, pay close attention to horizontal cracks in block walls, step cracks that continue to change, or cracks that coincide with active seepage during or soon after heavy rain. Hairline vertical cracks can sometimes be non-structural, but if moisture is present (tide-like seepage, staining, wet tracks, or bubbling efflorescence), it means the crack is acting as a leak path. If a crack is structural or shows signs of movement, a structural engineer assessment is often recommended before sealing. Ask your contractor whether the repair will be epoxy (structural/stable) or polyurethane (active leak), and how perimeter drainage will be addressed.

How much does foundation crack repair cost in View Royal?

For many typical homes in the Vancouver Island and Coast market, foundation crack repair by injection is commonly quoted in the $250 – $800 band, assuming a limited number of cracks and manageable access. The true cost can rise when the scope expands to include crack mapping, extensive surface prep, additional drilling ports, or when the contractor recommends complementary interior drainage because hydrostatic pressure is still pushing water toward the wall. In practice, owners often start with injection because it’s a focused fix, but if rainfall-driven groundwater keeps building pressure, you’ll usually need an interior perimeter drain/sump or exterior drainage correction as well. Always get a written breakdown so you can see what’s included versus what’s excluded.

Do I need a sump pump in View Royal?

You may need a sump pump in View Royal when interior drainage collects significant groundwater, when seepage continues during rainy periods, or when the basement floor stays wet despite a perimeter drain channel. Coastal BC conditions can keep the soil saturated longer, increasing the likelihood that water levels rise enough to require active pumping. A sump becomes even more important when the discharge route and hydrostatic pressure design require reliable removal of water. Many owners choose a sump pump system with battery backup because spring and storm events can coincide with power interruptions. Pricing for sump installation and backup often lands in the $800 – $2,500 range, depending on pump type and whether backup is included.

How does View Royal's soil affect my foundation?

Soil affects both how much water reaches your foundation and how much pressure that water can exert. In many Canadian regions with water-retentive soils, clay can hold moisture and increase lateral pressure against foundation walls; coastal sites on Vancouver Island can stay saturated longer after rainfall, which extends the period of damp conditions. Even if you don’t have “moving” soil, saturated backfill and groundwater can create hydrostatic pressure that finds weak points like cracks, mortar joints, or older weeping tile connections. In View Royal, it’s common for older homes (including the pre-1981 stock) to have drainage components that have aged, making soil saturation more damaging over time. The best way to confirm impact is an inspection of drainage performance, crack elevations, and any evidence of recurring efflorescence or mould.
