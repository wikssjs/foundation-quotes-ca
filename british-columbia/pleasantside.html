Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit, especially when the work affects load‑bearing elements or alters how water leaves the property. In many Pleasantside basement projects, a permit is also triggered when you connect or modify discharge routes for drainage systems—particularly if a sump pump installation requires tie‑ins or approvals for how water is handled.

Structural cracks are a special case. If you have horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracks, or evidence the foundation has moved, an engineer’s assessment is often required to determine whether underpinning or other structural work is needed before sealing and water management begin. That’s why you should ask your contractor whether they provide engineering support for structural repairs, and confirm they carry liability insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage for their workers.

Step-by-step verification for a Pleasantside homeowner:

  • Request the contractor’s full business name and confirm the current licence/registration status through the appropriate online provincial contractor registry.
  • Ask for a certificate of liability insurance and verify it covers foundation/waterproofing activities and is active on your job start date.
  • Ask for WSIB (or WCB) clearance for their workers—don’t accept “we’re insured” without paperwork.
  • Confirm in writing whether your scope requires permits and who will pull them.
  • For structural crack work, ask for the engineer’s written scope/plan before injection or wall sealing.

Work that often does not typically require a permit includes general housekeeping waterproofing measures like installing or cleaning downspout extensions, correcting surface grading for runoff direction, and adding non‑structural window well drainage—however, if you’re changing discharge routes or doing structural crack repair, always verify before you start.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Pleasantside need?

In Pleasantside and the broader Lower Mainland–Southwest market, the most important decision is whether to address the water before it reaches the foundation (exterior) or manage it after it enters (interior). Exterior waterproofing involves full excavation, a new exterior membrane, and new drainage tile around the foundation perimeter, followed by backfill and regrading. Done correctly, it targets the source and reduces hydrostatic pressure at the wall. The trade‑off is higher cost and landscape disruption—deck removal, curb/sidewalk adjustments, and more excavation time in tight yards.

Interior waterproofing is different. It typically uses a perimeter drain channel along the inside edge, a sump pit, and a sump pump to capture seepage and keep the basement dry. It’s less invasive and often ideal when you can’t excavate due to landscaping, fences, or finished areas. But interior systems don’t stop the exterior wall from being exposed to pressure; they “manage” it. In Lower Mainland–Southwest conditions—wet winters, persistent saturation, and freeze‑thaw expanding joints—interior-only solutions work best when combined with proper crack injection and when the sump is sized correctly.

Which approach fits your home depends on foundation type. Poured‑concrete walls generally respond well to crack injection as part of a broader moisture plan, while block foundations commonly benefit from interior drainage as a practical complement—especially when original weeping tile is undersized or has failed. Also consider backup: British Columbia’s spring storms can create conditions where power loss happens right when rain loads spike. A battery backup or equivalent strategy can be the difference between a damp day and a flood.

For example, if you’re choosing between full exterior excavation and an interior drain approach, exterior can commonly run in the $15,000–$30,000 band, while interior perimeter drainage often fits around $8,000–$18,000. The extra cost is usually justified when water pressure is persistent (continuous dampness, high groundwater indicators) or when failed drainage tile is suspected—situations where interior-only work may not keep walls and floors from re-saturating between storms.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Pleasantside

Choosing the right contractor in Pleasantside starts with verifying the basics in British Columbia: licence/registration status, liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB coverage. Ask for the certificate of insurance and confirm the coverage is active; then request proof of WSIB/WCB or a clearance document for their workers. If a contractor can’t produce these documents promptly, it’s a red flag—waterproofing delays can quickly turn into ongoing moisture damage, and you don’t want uncovered liability if something goes wrong.

Next, request 2–3 itemised written quotes. Ideally, you want a breakdown that separates labour and materials (membrane/drain tile/pipe, excavation and disposal, sump pit and pump components, crack injection materials, and reinstatement). Avoid quotes that only provide a single lump sum without exclusions and assumptions—ask what’s included for permit pulls, disposal, and any reinstatement like landscaping or patching.

Warranty matters in the Lower Mainland–Southwest. Ask for two layers: a workmanship warranty length for installation, and a product/manufacturer warranty for membranes, drain components, and pumps. Confirm whether warranties are transferable if you sell the home.

Payment schedule should be conservative. Never pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back the remaining balance until the job is complete and functional (including a test of sump operation if a sump is part of the scope). Finally, insist on a written timeline: start date, weather-related scheduling expectations, and an estimated completion window.

Can I waterproof my basement from the inside only?

Yes, you can waterproof from the inside only in many Pleasantside homes, and it’s often the best choice when excavation isn’t practical. Interior perimeter drains, a sump pit, and a properly sized sump pump manage water after it enters—reducing floor saturation and helping prevent recurring seepage in finished basements. What causes foundation cracks in Pleasantside?

Foundation cracks in Pleasantside usually come from a combination of movement and moisture effects. In the Lower Mainland–Southwest, persistent saturation and freeze‑thaw cycles are big contributors: water enters through joints or hairline cracks, then expands during cold snaps, widening the opening. Over time, this accelerates deterioration around penetrations (pipes, weep holes, and seams). Soil moisture and drainage issues can also increase lateral pressure against basement walls, especially where original perimeter drainage is undersized or failing. The type of foundation matters too—poured concrete cracks often behave differently than block or stone, and older homes may have older waterproofing systems that allowed more moisture to reach the foundation over many years. If you’re seeing horizontal cracks, step cracking in block, or cracks that worsen after storms, treat that as a potential structural indicator—an assessment may be required before you seal anything. For budgeting, foundation crack repair is commonly in the $500–$2,000 range when injection is appropriate, but the full plan depends on whether water pressure remains active.

What is a weeping tile and does my Pleasantside home have one?

Weeping tile (often called perimeter drain tile) is a drainage system installed around the foundation to collect groundwater and seepage and route it away—typically toward a sump pit or an outlet. In many older Pleasantside and Lower Mainland–Southwest homes, the original weeping tile may still be present but may have failed due to age, root intrusion, sediment buildup, or undersized drainage capacity. That’s why you’ll sometimes see interior seepage even when exterior waterproofing was “good enough” at the time: the tile no longer captures groundwater reliably. Because your home’s build date and foundation type influence what’s been installed, you usually can’t confirm from appearance alone—contractors confirm by inspection and, when appropriate, camera or probe testing. If you suspect a failed system, interior drainage and sump installation may be needed first, or an exterior perimeter replacement may be recommended to restore source control. Many homeowners end up deciding between interior work in the $8,000–$18,000 range and exterior drainage replacement in the $15,000–$30,000 band based on whether the weeping tile is functioning and whether groundwater pressure is persistent.
