Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit. That often includes work that alters how water is directed from the property (for example, modifying discharge routing for drainage tile or changing grades around the foundation). Sump pump installations also commonly require municipal approval when the discharge connects to storm or sanitary systems, or when the discharge path must be altered to meet local requirements. For structural crack repair—particularly horizontal cracks in block walls or major step cracks—an assessment by a structural engineer is often required to determine whether underpinning, partial structural repair, or other measures are needed before sealing.

From a homeowner standpoint in Armstrong, the practical step is to verify the contractor can support these requirements. Ask whether they will pull the permit (if applicable) and whether their permit process includes any required engineering sign-offs. Also confirm they carry liability insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage (or the appropriate coverage for the contractor’s work type). For structural repairs, ask if they provide engineering support on their team or coordinate directly with an engineer you can review.

To verify, check three items: (1) their business and contractor licensing details through the appropriate BC registry online, (2) a current certificate of insurance for liability and proof of workers’ coverage, and (3) any permit/engineering clearance letter they provide for structural scope items. If they can’t show current paperwork before starting, that’s a red flag—waterproofing is not a “guess and patch” trade, especially where cracks suggest possible movement.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Armstrong need?

Exterior waterproofing and interior waterproofing solve different problems. Exterior waterproofing involves full excavation, surface preparation, new membrane, and new drainage tile/backfill. It’s the most complete way to address the source of water entry, but it’s disruptive and more expensive because excavation and reinstatement are unavoidable. Interior waterproofing typically uses a perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump to collect water after it enters the foundation. It generally costs less and causes less landscape disruption, but it does not stop hydrostatic pressure from acting on the foundation wall; it manages it.

In Armstrong’s Thompson–Okanagan conditions, the right choice often depends on how water is getting in. Where homes have older drainage details, mixed sand/silt with clay pockets, and visible seepage along slab edges or cold joints, the water path may be coming from outside the foundation line. In that scenario, exterior work is more likely to be justified—especially when older weeping tile is compromised—often aligning with the $18,000–$40,000 exterior excavation band. If your basement is only showing localized dampness, efflorescence, or a limited number of active cracks—and the rest of the exterior system is functioning—an interior perimeter drain and sump approach can be a good fit, typically within the $9,000–$22,000 interior band.

Foundation material matters too. Poured concrete walls often respond better to targeted crack injection, particularly when cracks are non-moving or can be stabilized. Block foundations frequently benefit from interior drainage because water can travel through block voids and mortar joints, making interior capture a practical complement to crack sealing. Also consider sump pump backup: spring storms can trigger power interruptions just when seepage peaks, so backup systems can prevent repeat flooding and odours.

Example: if you have a short crack run and no exterior drainage signs, paying for crack injection plus interior sealing may cost a few thousand and avoid a large excavation. But if you’re replacing failing weeping tile or correcting a long exterior water path, the extra investment is usually justified because it reduces the chances you’ll re-open interior work after one or two wet seasons.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Armstrong

Start by confirming your waterproofing contractor’s credentials in British Columbia, then verify the insurance that protects you during demolition. Licensing requirements can vary by scope, but you should still be able to provide proof of business registration/licence and current insurance. For liability insurance, ask for a certificate that lists you/your property as additionally insured where possible, and confirm coverage limits are appropriate for excavation and concrete work. For WSIB/WCB coverage, ask for proof of workers’ compensation coverage for the workers who will be on site—don’t accept “we have someone insured” as an answer. If a contractor can’t produce these documents before starting, walk away.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes, not a single lump sum. You want a labour + materials breakdown showing: excavation/disposal (if exterior), membrane/drainage components, sump pit and discharge details (if applicable), crack injection type and crack footage/linear metres, and surface reinstatement. Read exclusions: is permit pulling included, is engineering required included (if cracks suggest movement), and is hauling/disposal included for removed concrete/asphalt and unsuitable backfill? Make sure the quote states who provides the product data sheets and whether the warranty is workmanship-only or product + workmanship.

On warranty, ask for the workmanship warranty length and confirm whether it transfers if you sell the home. For payment schedule, never pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until key milestones are complete (for example, before backfill and after final discharge checks). Finally, insist on a written timeline with a start date and completion estimate—waterproofing schedules can slip, but vague promises lead to stalled basements and open excavations in our freeze-thaw seasons.

Do I need a permit for foundation work in British Columbia?

Often, yes. In British Columbia, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes that alter lot drainage typically require a building permit. Sump pump work may also require municipal approval if discharge connects to municipal storm or sanitary systems or if discharge routing changes from what’s currently approved. How long does waterproofing last?

Longevity depends on whether you solved the water entry path or only managed water after it enters. Exterior waterproofing systems—when done with proper membrane, correct drainage tile installation, and good backfill—are designed for long service life, but they still require periodic maintenance of discharge outlets (like ensuring downspouts and surface drainage stay clear). Interior systems typically last well when the sump is correctly sized and maintained, and when backup power is available for peak wet periods. Crack injection can last many years when the crack is stable, but if the crack is actively moving due to pressure from clay-heavy soils or poor drainage, you can see recurring leaks. In Armstrong, with freeze-thaw cycles, it’s common for short-term “sealing only” to be less durable than source-focused repairs.

Can I waterproof my basement from the inside only?

Often you can manage seepage from the inside, especially when the leak is localized and you don’t have easy exterior access. Interior perimeter drains, a sump pit, and sealing can reduce water accumulation and improve basement comfort. But interior-only solutions don’t stop hydrostatic pressure from acting on the foundation wall. In Armstrong’s Thompson–Okanagan context—where clay pockets can hold water longer and older weeping tile may be failed—interior-only work can sometimes become a “repeat maintenance” scenario if the exterior water path remains active. If your quotes are only discussing an interior drain and sump, ask whether they assessed exterior drainage/weep tile function and whether mould or efflorescence remediation is needed before sealing.

What causes foundation cracks in Armstrong?

Common causes in Armstrong include freeze-thaw movement (widening small gaps over time), soil expansion in clay pockets, and long-term settlement effects—especially in older homes. If 42.7% of Armstrong homes were built before 1981 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), older foundation details may be more susceptible to joint deterioration and drainage failure. Poor lot grading, clogged downspouts, or failing exterior weeping tile can also increase moisture around the foundation, which accelerates cracking. The key question isn’t only “why is there a crack,” but “is it moving?” That affects whether an epoxy (for stable cracks) or polyurethane (for active leaks) approach is appropriate, and whether you should involve a structural engineer.

How do I compare waterproofing quotes?

Compare quotes line-by-line. Make sure each quote describes the same scope: exterior excavation vs interior drain and sump, the chosen crack injection product (epoxy vs polyurethane), sump discharge route, and whether backup power is included. Also compare disruption and reinstatement: excavation often pushes you into the $18,000–$40,000 exterior range, while interior perimeter drainage and sump work commonly falls within the $9,000–$22,000 band. Crack repair-only can look small (often around the $800–$3,500 range), but only if the crack is stable and the water entry path is addressed. Finally, verify permit responsibility, disposal, and warranty transferability in writing. The best quote is usually the one that matches the actual failure mechanism—not the lowest number.
