Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit, particularly when work affects load-bearing elements or involves modifications that can alter how water is managed around the foundation. If you’re installing a sump pump, connecting discharge to a storm or sanitary system usually requires municipal approval and may need documentation showing the discharge method and overflow considerations. In older Esquimalt homes, we frequently see contractors recommend perimeter drainage upgrades after inspection—those exterior drainage changes are often the trigger for permits because they alter the lot’s drainage behaviour.

For structural crack repairs (for example, horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracks, or cracks suggesting movement), a structural engineer’s assessment is often required to confirm whether underpinning or other structural work is needed. Before sealing anything, a reputable contractor should be able to point to engineer involvement where warranted and provide a plan that matches what the permit application will describe.

Homeowners can verify compliance step-by-step: (1) ask for the contractor’s licence/business registration details and check the relevant provincial registry online; (2) request a current certificate of insurance naming you as the certificate holder if possible; (3) confirm WSIB/WCB coverage if the contractor is employing workers; and (4) for permit-required work, confirm who will pull the permit and whether they provide the permit number and inspection schedule. If a contractor can’t provide clear documentation, pause and request it in writing.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Esquimalt need?

The fundamental difference is where the water problem is controlled. Exterior waterproofing—full excavation, new membrane, new drainage tile, and re-installation of backfill—targets the source. It’s more expensive and disruptive because it involves excavation around the entire perimeter and careful regrading afterward. Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump—doesn’t stop hydrostatic pressure from building against the wall; instead, it collects water after it enters and routes it to a pump. On Vancouver Island and the Coast, where saturated backfill can remain wet for extended periods after rainfall, interior-only fixes can work well when the water entry is limited or when the main issue is seepage at the slab edge. But when perimeter drainage is fully failed, interior systems can become “catch-and-carry” solutions that cost more over time.

In Esquimalt, poured-concrete walls commonly respond well to crack injection as part of an overall strategy, because the crack can be stabilized and sealed once water entry is controlled. Block foundations often need interior drainage as a practical complement because mortar joints and uneven surfaces can create multiple seepage points; in those cases, a perimeter drain channel and sump system reduce the ongoing moisture load even if sealing is done. Because British Columbia homes can experience power interruptions during heavy spring weather and localized outages, backup pumps are a smart add-on rather than an afterthought.

A good example: if you have a single damp corner with active seepage and a short run to repair the crack, crack injection plus an interior drain channel might be justified near the lower interior band. But if inspection shows water is entering all along the perimeter after storms—especially with older weeping tile failure—the price gap can be justified: moving from interior-only work toward the exterior backbone of $7,000–$18,000 can reduce the chance you’ll be paying for repeated interior remedies within a few years.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Esquimalt

Start by verifying that the contractor operating in Esquimalt is properly licensed for the work they’re proposing and that they carry liability insurance plus WSIB/WCB coverage if they employ workers. In practice, you’ll want a copy of the contractor’s insurance certificate (ask it to show your address or to include you as certificate holder when appropriate) and confirmation of WSIB/WCB clearance. Then, ask directly who pulls the permit and who is responsible for the warranty documentation for both workmanship and products. For complex structural crack work, make sure they acknowledge when engineering support is needed and can provide the relevant documentation trail.

When pricing, get 2–3 itemised, written quotes rather than lump sums. The breakdown should show labour hours, excavation/dewatering assumptions, membrane and drainage materials, sump components (including backup, if specified), disposal/restoration allowances, and whether test/drain verification is included. Read the exclusions line-by-line: for example, is permit pull included, is disposal of excavated soil included, and does the quote include repairs to landscaping or only rough backfill? A solid warranty should specify workmanship duration, product/manufacturer warranty details, and whether it’s transferable to future owners.

Payment schedule matters: never pay more than 10–15% upfront. Hold back a portion until completion and final cleanup are verified. Also request the start date and completion estimate in writing so you can compare timelines across contractors fairly.
