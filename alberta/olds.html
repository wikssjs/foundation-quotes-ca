Permits & regulations in Alberta

In Alberta, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit. If the scope includes significant alteration to drainage patterns around the home (for example, redirecting downspouts, altering grading, or adding major swales), it’s commonly reviewed under municipal permitting processes. Where work crosses into structural repair—such as repairing major horizontal cracks in block walls, addressing significant step cracking, or any situation where wall stability may be affected—an assessment by a structural engineer is often needed to confirm whether underpinning or related structural work is required.

Sump pump installations can also trigger approval steps depending on how discharge is routed. Connecting a discharge line to storm or sanitary systems generally needs municipal approval; otherwise, many projects use discharge to a safe surface route (based on local requirements and site conditions). Always ask the contractor what permits they will pull and what they consider “excluded.”

To verify an Olds contractor before you sign: (1) confirm they’re listed appropriately via the Alberta registry for the trade scope they claim, (2) request a current certificate of insurance showing general liability (and verify limits), (3) obtain proof of WSIB/WCB coverage clearance where applicable, and (4) for structural repair scopes, confirm they have documented engineering support and liability coverage for their work. Step-by-step, you should receive insurance documents before work starts—certificates often show effective dates and project-relevant coverage.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Olds need?

In Olds basements, exterior waterproofing and interior waterproofing are not “either/or” in a simple way—they answer different parts of the water problem. Exterior waterproofing (full excavation, new membrane, new drainage tile, and controlled backfill) addresses the source by managing water pressure before it reaches the wall. It costs more and usually requires landscape disruption, but it’s the most complete way to reduce future seepage when the foundation is exposed to clay-saturated soil and meltwater. Interior waterproofing (a perimeter drain channel, sump pit, and sump pump) manages water after it enters. It’s typically less invasive, can be done with less exterior access, and it’s often a practical match for basements where exterior excavation is hard due to patios, decks, or tight lot lines.

Given Olds’s freeze–thaw cycles and clay-heavy soil, many homes benefit from a systems approach: crack sealing plus interior drainage when the source can’t be fully controlled from outside. Poured concrete walls often respond well to crack injection when cracks are stable; polyurethane can be used when there’s active leakage. Block foundations, on the other hand, frequently require interior drainage as a practical complement because joints and mortar lines can become persistent seepage paths under pressure.

Sump backup matters in Alberta because spring flooding and storm events can coincide with power outages. A battery or water-powered backup prevents the common “water comes in faster than the pump can run” scenario when power is lost.

As a dollar example, if you’re choosing between interior perimeter drainage (often in the $5,000–$15,000 band) and full exterior excavation (often in the $9,000–$25,000 band), the higher price is justified when you have recurring seepage, multiple leaking sections, and exterior access is feasible. If the leak is isolated and most of the exterior can’t be accessed without major restoration, interior drainage and targeted crack work often provides the best value.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Olds

Choosing the right waterproofing contractor in Olds starts with verifying that they’re properly covered and able to stand behind the work. In Alberta, always request proof of general liability (certificate of insurance), and ask about WSIB/WCB coverage or clearance documentation where applicable. If the company is doing structural crack repair or any work that could affect stability, confirm they have engineering support and that their insurance applies to the scope you’re purchasing. You can also ask them for the clearance letter number and verify coverage is active by checking the document dates and the named insured.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes. “Itemised” means labour and materials broken down—membrane type, drainage tile, sump and controls, piping and fittings, disposal and restoration—so you can compare apples to apples. Read exclusions carefully: whether permit pull is included, whether disposal of clay spoil is included, whether concrete saw-cutting and patching is part of the price, and whether window well drains or downspout redirection are included or treated as add-ons.

Warranty matters. Ask for (1) the workmanship warranty length and what it covers, (2) the manufacturer warranty for specific products, and (3) whether the warranty is transferable if you sell your home. For payment, avoid large deposits—never more than 10–15% upfront. Hold back the remainder until the job is complete and inspected. Finally, insist on a written start date and completion timeline so you can plan around excavation, interior floor work, and exterior restoration.

Frequently asked questions — waterproofing in Olds

What causes foundation cracks in Olds?

In Olds and the wider Calgary area, foundation cracks are usually triggered by movement and water—especially in clay-heavy soils. When soils saturate and then freeze, freeze–thaw expands moisture and increases pressure on basement walls and footings. That can widen existing hairline cracks and open mortar lines over time, particularly in homes built before 1981 when older waterproofing systems and weeping-tile drainage were designed for different conditions. Another common cause is poor runoff management: if downspouts discharge near the foundation, repeated wetting dries, then re-wets, which stresses concrete and mortar joints. If you’re seeing horizontal cracks or persistent seepage, don’t just inject—ask how they’ll determine whether the crack is stable or actively moving, since that affects the product choice and scope.

How do I compare waterproofing quotes?

Start by comparing scope, not just totals. Ask each contractor to provide an itemised breakdown (labour, materials, pump/membrane specifics, pipe sizes, disposal, and restoration). For Olds basements, make sure they address the real water path: whether the plan is exterior source control (membrane + drainage tile) or interior management (perimeter drain + sump). A quote for interior drainage may land in the $5,000–$15,000 band, while exterior excavation often sits in the $9,000–$25,000 range—so if one quote includes exterior membrane and the other doesn’t, that’s a different job. Also confirm whether permits are included and whether backup power is proposed for the sump. Lastly, compare warranties: workmanship terms, product/manufacturer coverage, and whether the warranty is transferable.

How long does basement waterproofing take in Olds?

Timing depends on whether you’re doing exterior excavation or interior drainage, and on access constraints typical in Olds. Interior perimeter drain work (with a sump pit and pump) often takes a few days of active construction plus cure/patch time, but scheduling can be longer if there’s foundation crack injection preparation or mould/efflorescence remediation first. Exterior work usually takes longer because excavation, membrane installation, drainage tile placement, and backfilling must be sequenced correctly to avoid trapping water in clay backfill. Weather also matters: extreme cold can affect cure times and membrane handling. In practice, many projects run longer than homeowners expect if excavation uncovers unexpected conditions (failed weeping tile, soft spots, or heavy clay spoil that needs careful disposal). A reputable contractor will give you a written start date and completion estimate tied to their sequence.

What is a weeping tile and does my Olds home have one?

Weeping tile is a drainage system installed around the foundation to collect seepage and redirect water away from the basement. In many older Olds homes—especially those built before 1981—there may be original weeping tile, but it may be partially clogged, disconnected, or collapsed. You can’t always see it without excavation because tile is typically buried in backfill and connected to a sump or discharge point (or it drained by gravity). Signs you likely have a failing weeping-tile system include recurring dampness during spring runoff, staining along foundation wall lines, and efflorescence that returns even after surface cleaning. Some homeowners discover their weeping tile exists only to find it’s ineffective. A contractor should be able to explain what inspection methods they use (visual from cleanouts or limited exploratory openings) and how that affects whether you need interior drains, sump upgrades, or exterior replacement.

Can I waterproof my basement in winter in Olds?

It’s often possible, but winter waterproofing is limited by temperature, moisture conditions, and curing requirements. In Olds, January cold can make excavation and membrane work slower, and it can affect how certain materials bond and cure. Interior work may still be done because basement floors and wall surfaces can be kept workable with proper planning and temporary heating, but contractors must manage condensation risk and ensure surfaces are dry enough for sealing and crack injection products. If you have active leaks, the team may need to address water first (for example, installing a sump or managing discharge) before sealing. Many contractors prefer late spring or early fall for full exterior excavation to avoid freeze restrictions and to support reliable backfill compaction. If winter work is your only option, ask the contractor to describe how they’ll control temperature and drying, and confirm warranty coverage for cold-weather application.

What is the difference between waterproofing and damp-proofing?

Waterproofing is designed to prevent water entry or manage hydrostatic pressure effectively, while damp-proofing focuses on resisting minor moisture and condensation. In Olds, where clay soils can hold water and freeze–thaw can reopen cracks, true waterproofing is usually the correct standard for recurring seepage. Exterior membrane and drainage tile systems are typically considered waterproofing because they address the source and pressure. Interior drainage with a perimeter drain channel and sump is also a waterproofing strategy because it relieves water pressure after entry, especially when combined with a reliable sump pump and, ideally, backup power. Damp-proofing might help with early, low-grade moisture but often fails when there’s sustained lateral pressure and active cracking. If you’re budgeting, interior waterproofing commonly falls into the $5,000–$15,000 range, while exterior excavation often goes higher (for example $9,000–$25,000) because it targets water entry at the foundation.
