Permits & regulations in Alberta

In Alberta, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit. If the work involves altering how water is managed on-site—especially when it affects how drainage leaves the property—your contractor should confirm permit requirements before starting. Sump pump installations that connect to municipal storm or sanitary sewer systems generally need municipal approval. For structural crack repair (for example, major horizontal cracks in block walls, or cracking patterns that suggest movement), a structural engineer’s assessment is often required to determine whether underpinning or other structural work is needed.

Before you sign anything, verify your contractor has engineering support (when structural repairs are suspected), liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB coverage. Here’s a homeowner-friendly way to check, step by step:

  • Ask for the contractor’s Alberta licence/registration details and the legal name they use on invoices.
  • Request a current Certificate of Insurance and confirm it matches the company name on the quote.
  • Confirm WSIB/WCB coverage by asking for proof of clearance or account coverage documents.
  • For structural crack work, ask whether they coordinate with an engineer and include engineer recommendations in the scope.
  • Ask if permit pull is included, and who applies (contractor vs. homeowner).
  • If a sump discharges to a connection point, ask whether municipal approval is required and how it will be obtained.

Typically does not require a permit for most homeowners’ situations: interior sealing of non-structural, hairline cracks only; installation of a perimeter drain with a sump where discharge method stays within common on-site practices; and targeted window well drainage—but your contractor should confirm based on your discharge routing and foundation conditions.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Lamont need?

Exterior waterproofing is the “source control” approach: full excavation, new membrane, new drainage tile, and careful backfill. It permanently addresses where water enters by intercepting groundwater and sealing the exterior wall. The trade-off is cost and disruption—excavation is labour-intensive, and in Lamont that can mean landscaping, decks, or driveways must be removed and reinstated.

Interior waterproofing—perimeter drain channel, sump pit, sump pump, and often crack sealing—focuses on “managed entry”: it collects water after it reaches the basement and pumps it away. It’s typically less invasive, but it does not stop hydrostatic pressure against the wall itself. In clay-rich soils, this distinction matters: interior solutions work best when combined with proper crack injection and drainage that keeps water from saturating the slab area for long periods.

What’s “right” in Lamont depends on foundation type and leak pattern. Poured concrete walls often respond well to targeted crack injection (when cracks are non-moving), because concrete typically seals more predictably once the water path is properly sealed. Block foundations more often need interior drainage as a practical complement because mortar joints and block edges can act like pathways—especially when older weeping tile systems have failed or are undersized. Given spring melt events and freeze–thaw cycling, a sump pump backup system becomes important for reliability; battery backup helps when power flickers or outages occur while clay soils remain saturated.

Here’s a dollar example to illustrate trade-offs. If your issue is localized seepage through a few joints and hairline cracks, crack injection plus a sump upgrade might stay in the $600–$2,500 crack band plus the $2,100–$3,500 sump budget—often far less than exterior excavation. But if water is actively entering along multiple wall sections and the outside drainage is clearly failed, the exterior backbone range of $14,000–$22,000 is commonly justified because it removes the repeated “water pushing” cycle.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in Lamont

Choosing the right waterproofing contractor in Lamont is less about flashy marketing and more about verification and clarity. In Alberta, start by confirming licensing/registration, liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB coverage. Ask for documents before you sign: the insurer certificate should match the legal company name on your quote, and WSIB/WCB clearance or coverage proof should be current. If you’re dealing with structural cracks, insist on engineering support and a documented plan for how they’ll determine whether underpinning or structural remediation is required.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes instead of lump sums. A solid quote breaks down labour and materials (membrane type, drainage components, sump basin/piping, pump model and backup option, restoration items), and clearly lists exclusions such as mould remediation or disposal. Confirm whether permit pulling is included—if it’s required—and whether backfill disposal and equipment access are included or billed separately.

Warranty matters in Alberta’s climate. Ask for: (1) the workmanship warranty length, (2) product/manufacturer warranty details, and (3) whether the warranty is transferable if you sell the property. Payment schedule is another tell: never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and hold back part of the balance until the job is complete and verified. Finally, require a timeline in writing: start date, expected completion, and key milestones (e.g., excavation completion, membrane install, drain connection, interior drying/cleanup).

If they won’t put scope, warranty, and permit responsibility in writing, keep looking.

What is a battery backup sump pump and do I need one in Lamont?

A battery backup sump pump is an emergency power system that keeps the pump running when your main electrical power is interrupted. In Alberta—especially during spring melt—outages or brownouts can coincide with peak water inflow, and you don’t want your basement sitting under hydrostatic pressure while the soil stays saturated. Battery backup typically works alongside the primary sump pump and starts when the main power fails. Whether you “need” one depends on your leak history and how quickly water collects in your basement. If you’ve experienced dampness during power disturbances, or you have a clay-rich basement with active perimeter seepage, backup is a practical safeguard. When comparing quotes, ask if backup is included and what type it is (battery vs other solutions). Why is my basement leaking in Lamont?

Basement leaks in Lamont are usually the result of water getting through cracks or joints and then being held against the foundation by saturated soils. Clay-to-clay-loam subsoils can retain water, increasing lateral pressure on foundation walls during freeze–thaw cycles, which then enlarges existing cracks at cold joints. Many older homes—especially those built before 1981—also have weeping tile systems that are undersized or failed, so water finds new paths through mortar joints, around footing areas, or through localized weak points like window well regions. Another common contributor is surface runoff: downspouts or grading that directs water toward the foundation during heavy storms keeps soil wet for longer periods. If you see seepage after spring melt or during heavy rains, that pattern strongly points to groundwater and freeze–thaw effects. A contractor should confirm with inspection and discussion of leak timing and crack locations before recommending exterior excavation versus interior drainage and sump upgrades.
