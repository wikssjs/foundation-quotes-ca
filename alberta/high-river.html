Permits & regulations in Alberta

In Alberta, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit. For example, if a contractor is removing a section of foundation to install or repair a perimeter drainage system, that’s often treated as a permit-triggering foundation/drainage alteration. Similarly, if the scope includes modifying drainage routing, creating a new discharge path, or altering how water leaves the site, a permit may be required.

Sump pump installations may also need municipal approval—especially when the system connects to storm or sanitary sewer infrastructure. Even when you’re not “changing plumbing inside,” connecting discharge improperly can create compliance and backflow issues.

For structural crack repair—particularly horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracking, or anything that suggests movement—an assessment by a structural engineer is often needed to determine whether underpinning or other structural work is required. A good waterproofing contractor will either have engineering support in-house or coordinate with an engineer and will clearly outline what’s structural vs. what’s water-management.

To verify a contractor in High River, start with their Alberta business licence status (the provincial licence/registration information available through online resources), then ask for a current certificate of insurance (liability coverage) and proof of WSIB/WCB coverage. For certificate checks, look for policy dates and insured operations. If the contractor handles structural work or coordinates engineer reviews, ask whether engineering documents are provided and stored as part of your project file.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does High River need?

The fundamental difference is simple: exterior waterproofing addresses the source of water entry by excavating around the foundation, installing a continuous membrane, adding/repairing perimeter drainage tile, and backfilling to restore proper slope. Interior waterproofing, by contrast, manages water after it enters by creating a perimeter drain channel, installing a sump pit and sump pump, and intercepting seepage before it collects on your slab or basement floor. The exterior approach usually costs more and involves yard disruption; the interior approach is less invasive, but it doesn’t fully relieve hydrostatic pressure on the wall itself.

In High River’s clay-prone conditions and freeze–thaw cycles, exterior solutions are often preferred when there’s evidence of persistent hydrostatic pressure—think wall seepage after storms/melt, recurring efflorescence, or a history of failed weeping tile. Poured concrete walls can respond well to a combination of crack injection and a well-designed exterior drainage plan; block foundations frequently benefit from interior drainage as a practical complement because water pathways through block and mortar joints can keep reappearing unless interception is reliable.

Sump pump backup systems are a smart add in Alberta spring seasons. If power drops during flooding or heavy snowmelt, backup reduces the chance of water rising faster than a single pump can keep up.

Here’s a typical budgeting example: if you have widespread exterior seepage and access is straightforward, exterior waterproofing may land around $9,500–$24,000. If the same home has only localized seepage at one wall segment and the rest of the perimeter is stable, an interior perimeter drain plus sump may be better value at roughly $6,000–$14,500—but only if the contractor confirms the likely water pathways and discharge plan through a proper site assessment.

How to choose a waterproofing contractor in High River

Start by verifying three things in Alberta: licensing/registration (where applicable for the contractor’s business activity), liability insurance, and WSIB/WCB coverage. To check liability insurance, ask for a current certificate of insurance and confirm the policy is active for your project period. For WSIB/WCB, request proof of coverage or a clearance letter (as applicable) and verify the contractor’s name matches the business on the quote. If structural repairs or engineering coordination are in the scope, ask how they ensure the engineer’s recommendations are implemented.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes that separate labour and materials (membrane, drainage tile, sump components, pumps, backfill, restoration, and any concrete cutting). A proper quote should state what is included and what is excluded: permits, disposal fees for clay spoil, replacing failed weeping tile segments, discharge line routing, and whether pump backup is included. Never accept a “lump sum” without showing how the plan addresses the water pathway you’re experiencing.

Warranty matters: confirm the workmanship warranty length and whether it’s tied to maintenance (like sump pump checks). Also ask for the manufacturer’s product warranty terms and whether they’re transferable to you if you sell your home. Payment schedule should protect you—never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and use a holdback until the job is complete and cleaned up. Finally, request a start date and completion estimate in writing, including weather-related contingencies common in southern Alberta.

What is a weeping tile and does my High River home have one?

Weeping tile is the perimeter drain system installed around a foundation to collect infiltrating water and route it away from the basement area. Older homes in High River (many from earlier construction) often have a version of weeping tile, but it may be clogged, broken, or disconnected—especially with clay soils that expand and keep pressure on the foundation edges. What is the difference between waterproofing and damp-proofing?

Damp-proofing generally means protecting against minor moisture, while waterproofing is intended to control water under pressure—especially when soil stays saturated and freeze–thaw cycles push water into joints and cracks. In High River, dampness can become a recurring “water entry” problem because clay soils hold moisture and the foundation is stressed by seasonal freeze–thaw. Waterproofing approaches like exterior membranes with functioning drainage tile or interior perimeter drains and sumps are designed to intercept and manage water that reaches the basement envelope. A damp-proofing coating alone may not handle hydrostatic pressure after heavy spring melt or during localized storm events. If you’re budgeting, a sump-based interior system often falls within ranges like $6,000–$14,500, while exterior excavation and membrane solutions are typically higher (often $9,500–$24,000) because they address the water source and pathway more comprehensively.
