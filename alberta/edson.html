Permits & regulations in Alberta

In Alberta, foundation excavation, structural crack repair, and changes to lot drainage typically require a building permit, especially when work could affect structural elements or how water is managed on site. Sump pump installations that connect to the storm or sanitary sewer usually require municipal approval before discharge is routed. If you’re dealing with horizontal cracks in block walls, major step cracks, or signs that the foundation is moving, a structural engineer’s assessment is often required to determine whether underpinning or other structural work is needed.

For your quote in Edson, confirm the contractor is set up for both the construction side and the risk side. Structural repairs should be supported by engineering documentation when required, and the contractor should carry liability insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage. Ask whether their design drawings and discharge plan align with permit requirements and where the discharge will go (surface to swale, soakaway, or approved connection).

To verify a contractor in Alberta, use a straightforward checklist: (1) check their licence/registration through the appropriate provincial registry for their trade category; (2) request a certificate of insurance and confirm it’s current and matches the work description; (3) ask for WSIB/WCB clearance or account proof and verify it’s active; and (4) request written confirmation of whether permits are included in the project scope. This is the fastest way to avoid “scope creep” once excavation reveals drainage failures or crack conditions that trigger additional requirements.

Interior vs exterior waterproofing — what does Edson need?

The fundamental difference in Edson is that exterior waterproofing tries to prevent water from entering the foundation, while interior waterproofing manages water after it enters. Exterior waterproofing usually means full excavation along the basement walls, removal of failed materials, installation of a new membrane, and a properly graded drainage tile system with controlled discharge—so it addresses the source of water entry and hydrostatic pressure. It costs more and disrupts landscaping, but in mountain/foothill conditions with seasonal high water tables, it’s often the most permanent fix when the perimeter drainage is truly failing.

Interior waterproofing typically involves a perimeter drain channel and sump pit/pump system, collecting seepage and reducing interior water accumulation. It’s less invasive, but it doesn’t stop hydrostatic pressure from building against the wall; it simply captures water once it appears. In this region, that matters because freeze–thaw cycles can widen existing crack pathways over time, meaning crack control and pumping capacity need to be matched to the site’s water behaviour.

For Edson homes with poured concrete walls, crack injection can be a strong complement: epoxy is suited for non-active cracks (often stable, structural-type sealing), while polyurethane is used for active leakage paths. For block foundations, interior drainage is frequently a practical complement because mortar joints and micro-channels can weep even when surfaces are treated. If you’re already seeing frequent spring seepage, pairing a perimeter system with pump upgrades—and adding battery backup—is sensible for resilience during spring high-water and potential outages.

Example: if your quote for interior perimeter drainage lands around $8,000–$22,000 but your exterior needs complete drainage replacement, exterior excavation may be $9,000–$25,000—the “extra” cost can be justified when exterior access is workable and you want source control rather than repeated pump-dependent management.

How do I know if a foundation crack is serious?

Not all cracks are serious, but in Edson you should take any crack seriously if it shows signs of movement or active leakage. A “serious” crack often includes horizontal cracks in block walls, step cracking, cracks that widen seasonally, or cracks accompanied by water seepage, dampness, mould growth, or efflorescence. Freeze–thaw can make previously stable cracks expand, so the behaviour over multiple seasons is a key indicator.

You can also monitor crack width changes and note where water appears during spring melt. If your crack is paired with recurring seepage even after pumping, the crack may be acting as a pathway that needs injection (epoxy for stable cracks or polyurethane for active leaks). If you see patterns suggesting structural movement, Alberta permitting and structural assessment may be required.
